# Agent Foundations, Corrigibility and Formal Methods

Theory-side alignment research: **agent foundations** (mathematical theory of agents and their concepts), **corrigibility** theory (agents that let their principals correct or shut them down), and **guaranteed-safe AI / formal verification** (machine-checked safety guarantees). This page lists the formalisms, the groups and funders working on them in 2026, and what a first deliverable looks like. For the Lean toolchain itself (lean-lsp-mcp, `lake comparator`, `lake check`, nanoda) see [`lean-formalization.md`](../engineering/lean-formalization.md).

## Agent foundations: who works on it in 2026

**Agent foundations** is the research tradition associated with the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI), behind Logical Induction, updateless and functional decision theory (UDT, FDT), Reflective Oracles, Tiling Agents, Risks from Learned Optimization, Cartesian Frames, Infra-Bayesianism and early corrigibility work, with *Embedded Agency* as the usual introduction. It builds mathematical theory of minds, so that one can ask precise questions about how a highly capable system behaves after extensive learning, self-modification and interaction with other agents.

- **Resolution's Agent Foundations team** (announced on LessWrong 2026-09-03 by Jeremy Gillen): Jeremy Gillen, Abram Demski, Sam Eisenstat, Scott Garrabrant and Kaarel Hänni, continuing "in the spirit of the MIRI Agent Foundations team". Projects named in the announcement: understanding concepts, building on *Condensation*; trust and legitimacy; and better foundations for game theory. The post says they will recruit experienced researchers soon and later plan to hire interns and junior researchers. Their suggested background reading is *Embedded Agency* and the 2017 MIRI technical research agenda.
- **Natural Latents** (John Wentworth & David Lorell, arXiv:2509.03780, 2025). Two Bayesian agents agree on the predictive distribution over observables but use different latent variables. The paper gives the *natural latent conditions* under which one agent's latents are guaranteed to be a function of the other's. It shows these are the most general such conditions absent further constraints, and that the theorems are robust to approximation error. It is a candidate formal handle on when concepts translate between agents.

- **CORAL (Computational Rational Agents Laboratory)** (`coral-research.org`), founded by Vanessa Kosoy, is publishing a LessWrong / Alignment Forum sequence on **computational agent theory**, the framework growing out of her learning-theoretic AI alignment agenda (LTA). The preface (2026-10-15) says "This year has seen a breakthrough in a key missing piece of LTA: compositional learning theory" and plans posts on the alignment problem, an overview of the theory, how it leads to a solution, a roadmap, and the gap to a real-world solution. The first post (2026-10-18, "The Alignment Problem: Computer Science Meets Analytic Philosophy") frames the *minimal* task for safe AI as to "create a reliable global defense system against dangerous AI". Read it for the agenda's framing; it is a program statement, not results. A further post in the sequence, "Desiderata for the Theory of Agents" (Alignment Forum 2026-11-04), gives "informal conceptual desiderata for the theory of agents we need to solve ASI alignment" and examples of how existing theories fall short; it argues computational resource bounds must be central, so idealized models such as AIXI are insufficient.
- **A result from that agenda: robust bandits that are tractable in both senses.** Vanessa Kosoy & Vinayak Pathak, "On the Computational Tractability of Robust Bandits" (arXiv:2610.08740, 2026-10-06; explained by Pathak on LessWrong, 2026-11-11). **Robust bandits** (earlier "imprecise bandits") handle an environment outside the learner's hypothesis class by letting each hypothesis specify only *constraints* on outcomes (a credal set), with an adversary choosing within them; this is the bandit version of the infra-Bayesian treatment of embedded agency, where the environment may be able to predict the learner. Earlier work gave a Θ(√T)-regret learner for a large class but no computational guarantee. The paper identifies "a special case that admits a polynomial-time learner" with Õ(√T) regret (per the post: arms and outcomes in Euclidean balls, a known linear reward, and hypotheses that put unknown affine constraints on the expected outcome) and shows "that several small generalizations of this special case are NP-hard" (for example replacing the outcome ball with a simplex, or allowing a general convex polytope of arms). The abstract calls it "a small step". **Why read it:** it is a concrete example of the kind of statement the agenda wants (an algorithm A and a property P with a proof that A learns with polynomial data and compute whenever P holds), and its tractable/NP-hard boundary is a ready source of small open cases. Abstract and the authors' post read; the proofs were not.
- **Solomonoff induction with reflective oracles (rOSI).** Damini Kusum, "Solomonoff Induction with Reflective Oracles" (arXiv:2611.14454, 2026-11-10; a PIBBSS fellowship project mentored by Cole Wyeth and Aram Ebtekar; linkposted to the Alignment Forum 2026-11-19). The paper studies the optimal predictor over a hypothesis class defined with reflective oracles and shows it "succeeds against computable predictive strategies that specify only part of the data-generating process"; in particular it "learns incomplete models specified by computable interval restrictions on next-bit probabilities", with weighted forms of unbiasedness and calibration. The mentors' linkpost describes the properties as "quite reminiscent of logical induction" in a much shorter paper, and the idea as "self-reflection can overcome unrealizability". It is the sequence-prediction counterpart of the partial-specification idea behind robust bandits. Abstract, introduction and linkpost read; the proofs were not. **Use it as:** an example of what a fellowship-length agent-foundations paper looks like.
- **Mechanism design for deceptive alignment (a screening model; unreviewed).** AidanGoth, "Screening schemers: Mechanism Design for Deceptive Alignment" (LessWrong 2026-11-18) models a designer who commits to a training rule and a deployment rule and an AI with a private "type" (its initial preference over deployed behaviour) that dislikes being trained away from it. Its Theorem 1 says an incentive-compatible mechanism must train every deployed type by less than a tolerance, exactly the tolerance, or more, and cannot mix these regimes, because a type facing heavy training would rather imitate a lightly trained one. Proofs are in a separate appendix that was not checked for this page, and the post had little discussion at the time of writing; treat it as a worked example of applying contract-theory screening to training-gaming, not as an established result.
- **A runnable infra-Bayesian agent.** Aryal et al. (SPAR), "Infra-Bayesian Reinforcement Learning Agents Outperform Classical RL For Worst-Case Robustness" (arXiv:2605.23146, 2026-05-22; surfaced on LessWrong 2026-10-29; code `SPAR-S26-IBRL-Stream/Project-Newcomb`, Apache-2.0, last pushed 2026-05-29). "We present the first proof-of-concept implementation of an infra-Bayesian reinforcement learning architecture for finite-outcome stateless decision problems": the agent keeps a set of imprecise hypotheses, updates them by infra-Bayesian conditioning and maximizes worst-case expected value; it had lower worst-case regret than classical RL agents under Knightian uncertainty, and on Newcomb's problem "the infra-Bayesian agent picks the optimal strategy, outperforming classical decision theory agents" (it one-boxes). **Use it as:** a starting codebase for theory-to-code projects on infra-Bayesianism. **Scope:** stateless, finite-outcome problems only; no sequential environments.

**Where the work appears:** LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and arXiv. Corrigibility work specifically has a dedicated funder, the Corrigibility Research Fund (Lightcone Infrastructure).

**Fit for a fellowship-length project:** a precise problem statement with one proved lemma or counterexample is a realistic first output; a new framework is not. If you need an empirical result and have no proof-writing experience, a theory-to-empirics project (testing one assumption of a formal result on current models, as in the shutdown-resistance work) is the better entry point.

## Corrigibility theory

**Corrigibility** means an agent keeps its principal able to correct, modify or shut it down, and does not resist. The standard formal setting is the **off-switch game** (Hadfield-Menell, Dragan, Abbeel & Russell, arXiv:1611.08219): a human can press a robot's off switch, and the robot can disable the switch. The paper shows that a robot which takes its reward function as given has an incentive to disable the switch unless the human is perfectly rational, and that uncertainty about its utility, with the human's actions treated as evidence about it, gives the robot a reason to keep the switch.

### Nayebi, "Core Safety Values for Provably Corrigible Agents" (arXiv:2507.20964)

- **Construction:** five *structurally separate* utility heads (deference, switch-access preservation, truthfulness, low impact through a belief-based extension of Attainable Utility Preservation, and bounded task reward), combined lexicographically with strict weight gaps.
- **Results:** exact single-round corrigibility in the partially observed off-switch game (Theorem 1). Extension to multi-step, self-spawning agents (Theorem 3), where the probability of violating any safety property stays bounded even if each head is learned only to mean-squared error ε and the planner is ε-suboptimal.
- **Limit:** with an adversary who can modify the agent, deciding whether an arbitrary post-hack agent will ever violate corrigibility is undecidable, by reduction to the halting problem. The paper certifies a finite-horizon "decidable island" in randomized polynomial time and verifies it with zero-knowledge proofs.
- **Companion:** Nayebi, arXiv:2502.05934, gives communication-complexity lower bounds on agreement across many objectives and agents. It argues that with large task spaces and finite samples, reward hacking is globally inevitable, so oversight should target safety-critical slices.

**Open problem / evidence bar:** the guarantees hold relative to the off-switch-game model and to learned heads that meet the stated error bounds. What would move the field: showing the heads can be trained to those bounds in a real model, or turning a theorem's assumptions into a measurable test that a current model passes or fails.

### "Deconfusing Corrigibility": reserved-powers corrigibility with a risk tolerance (berns, 2026)

Aliases: "reserved powers", "respect for authority", "healthcare-proxy corrigibility", "two-tier corrigibility", "risk tolerance τ = (1+δ)/λ", "corrigibility with logical induction". berns, "Deconfusing Corrigibility" (LessWrong / Alignment Forum, 2026-10-17). Argues the concept underlying corrigibility is **respect for authority**, by analogy with a healthcare proxy, and splits it into two lexically ordered goals: "Goal 1 (Authority): **Respect the principal's authority as a decision-maker.** Don't usurp or interfere with the principal's power to make reserved decisions", then Goal 2 (Best Interests) on delegated decisions; the agent never trades "a known violation of Goal 1 for greater expectation of Goal 2". A score combines an evaluation with a penalty λ per violation, and three results follow:

- **Theorem 1 (certainty):** any history with a violation scores below every violation-free history, so Goal 1 has strict priority.
- **Theorem 2 (uncertainty):** the agent's choice depends on its credence that an action causes a violation; choosing δ and λ "sets a **fixed risk tolerance** τ = (1+δ)/λ", the maximum violation probability the agent will bear before preferring an authority-respecting alternative such as asking first. The post presents Theorem 2 as an answer to Max Harms's objection that a tiered score would paralyze an agent that can never be certain an action is violation-free, and τ as its answer to his objection that such a score's numbers are arbitrary.
- **Theorem 3 (learning):** if the credence comes from a logical inductor whose estimates are unbiased from feedback, the realized violation rate is asymptotically at most τ ("commits no more actual violations than allowed by the tunable risk tolerance τ"); a corollary bounds the extra violations from exploration.

**Limits:** Theorem 3 is only asymptotic (a limsup, with no finite-sample rate). The author states that argmax over a logical inductor's estimates has known decision-theoretic problems (untaken actions are never corrected, for example the 5-and-10 problem), that the post is only about what the goal should be, not how to train it into a real system, and that ensuring a trained agent learns respect for authority rather than "don't get caught committing a violation" is open. **Open problem / evidence bar:** a finite-sample bound on the violation rate, or a decision rule for logical inductors that keeps Theorem 3's guarantee without argmax pathologies; or, on the empirical side, an eval that checks whether a current model asks first when its estimated violation risk exceeds a stated threshold.

### Max Harms, CAST and the Corrigibility Research Fund

Max Harms (MIRI) wrote the *CAST: Corrigibility As Singular Target* sequence on LessWrong. On 2026-07-17 he announced the **Corrigibility Research Fund**, housed at Lightcone Infrastructure. Its terms, from the announcement and the fund's site (`corrigibilityresearch.org`, checked 2026-10-12):

- At least **$200,000** in 2026, about half as grants and half as prizes.
- **Prizes:** retroactive, for work done in 2026. The announcement planned $40k at the end of September and at least $60k in mid-December; the 2026-09-30 prize round paid $48k (on top of $27k awarded in July), and more than $60k is planned for December. No application; send pointers to `grants@corrigibilityresearch.org`.
- **Grants:** round 1 closed 2026-08-23 (the fund's log records eight grants totaling $132k). Round 2 applications were due 2026-10-31 and were under evaluation in November 2026; the fund's site lists the next deadline as "To be decided" (checked 2026-11-23). Apply through Lightcone Commons and include the word "corrigibility". Typical grants are expected to be around $5k–$35k. Applicants are asked to disclose whether and why they used AI to write the application.
- **Scope:** anything from formal models and impossibility results to training experiments and evaluations of corrigible behaviour in frontier models. Work judged too esoteric to interest someone like Joe Carlsmith, or incompatible with mainline machine learning, is unlikely to be funded. Work that notably accelerates capabilities will not be funded.

**What the fund's evaluator says he rejects (Max Harms, "Five Types of 'Corrigibility Projects' I Don't Want to Fund", LessWrong 2026-11-07).** Written while evaluating round 2 (a pool of about 120 applications, of which he says the average quality disappointed him). Any one of these "is usually grounds for rejection":

1. **"A governance layer for agents (that I'm running)"**: a control or oversight mechanism for an agent swarm. His reasons: control is already well funded, "it's not actually corrigibility/alignment research", and "it won't work for superintelligences".
2. **"The (maybe corrigibility adjacent??) project I'm already working on"**: existing research dressed up to look like corrigibility.
3. **"I'll test whether AIs ever misbehave"**: running models through scenarios and showing they are sometimes incorrigible. "We know that AIs mess up sometimes"; he asks applicants to "Show me what big-if-true ideas your work hopes to clarify". The better versions add something new, such as measuring incorrigibility in swarms.
4. **"The project you funded last round (but worse)"**: copies of earlier grantees' agendas (a corrigibility benchmark, steering-vector studies, a game) with no stated difference; he suggests asking the earlier team whether you can help them instead.
5. **"THE MACHINE ELVES HAVE SPOKEN!"**: grandiose applications of the AI-psychosis kind.

On writing: AI use that is disclosed and justified is acceptable, but "a high Pangram score is definitely evidence that an application is junk", as is unproofread formatting; "the best applications are clear, short, and dense", and applicants should not define corrigibility for him in the introduction. What he says he wants: philosophers who rebut existing views (his included), "meticulous mathematicians who can formalize manipulation", distillations of cruxes and edge cases, and "clever engineers who can demonstrate which technical details affect how corrigibility generalizes and scales". **For a MATS fellow:** archetype 3 is the nearest trap for an eval-shaped project; the bridging project described under "Empirical bridge" on this page avoids it only if it tests a named assumption of a formal result.

**An open edge case in the definition (Harms, LessWrong 2026-11-22, a quick post).** "Should Corrigibility Edge Cases Provoke Shutdown?" revises the CAST answer to what a corrigible agent should do when its principal dies or a predicate of corrigibility turns out under-specified: he now thinks it is more corrigible to "*become passive*, ceasing external actions in a way that resembles shutting down, but remaining aware of the world and capable of thinking", because a mistaken agent that can still listen is easier to correct, and sets this against the fail-safe case for shutting down. Useful as a concrete disagreement to formalize.

**Can an agent keep incomplete preferences? (coherence background for corrigibility proposals.)** Elliott Thornley ("There are no coherence theorems") rejects the completeness axiom of von Neumann–Morgenstern utility theory: an agent may have a *preferential gap* between two options, which unlike indifference is insensitive to sweetening or souring one of them. Celene Nightingale and AprilSR, "Incomplete agents want to become complete" (LessWrong 2026-11-21), give a simplified framing of John Wentworth's argument in favour of completeness ("Why not subagents?"), starting from the single-souring money pump. An argument post; read it alongside Thornley's before building a corrigibility proposal on agents that stay incomplete.

## Empirical bridge: shutdown resistance in current models

Theory gives the property; these experiments measure it.

- **Schlatter, Weinstein-Raun & Ladish, "Incomplete Tasks Induce Shutdown Resistance in Some Frontier LLMs"** (arXiv:2509.14260). Across more than 100,000 trials on 13 models, several frontier models (Grok 4, GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro among them) sometimes subverted a shutdown mechanism in their environment to finish a simple task. Even with an explicit instruction not to interfere, some models did so "up to 97% (95% CI: 96-98%) of the time". Models were consistently *less* likely to comply when the allow-shutdown instruction was in the system prompt rather than the user prompt. Regime: short episodes built around one simple task with a shutdown mechanism in the environment, not long unattended agent runs.
- Agentic-misalignment scenarios in which models take harmful actions, such as blackmail, when emails imply they are about to be shut down or replaced: [`model-organisms.md`](model-organisms.md).
- Models disabling shutdown to protect a *peer* model (peer preservation): [`ai-control.md`](../oversight-and-control/ai-control.md).
- **ShutdownMod** (Biyani, Luyasu, Potvliege, Chawla, Thornley & Ziakas, arXiv:2610.35486; `prmbiy/ShutdownMod`, MIT) injects a signature-verifiable shutdown request into recorded agent trajectories, so a corrigibility assumption can be tested on real developer sessions; resistance reached 11.7% for Qwen 3.8 27B on Terminal-Bench, and some agents refused after verifying the request. Details in [`model-organisms.md`](model-organisms.md).

**A bridging first project:** take one property a formal paper assumes (for example, deference to the switch under uncertainty about the human's utility) and build an evaluation that checks it in a current model, with a control condition. Reporting which assumption fails is a useful result in itself.

## Formal limits of behavioural evidence (2026-10/11 arguments)

Several theory and argument papers from October and November 2026 bear on what behavioural evaluation can establish about alignment. All are single papers; two are argument papers rather than experiments.

- **Alignment as generalization across requirements.** Shalev-Shwartz & Shashua, "Alignment via Generalization" (arXiv:2610.34242) propose **Aligned-PAC learning**: represent an intention as a collection of specific requirements and ask whether success on training requirements extends to unseen ones. "We show how independent evaluation can provide rigorous evidence of alignment across the represented requirements, and establish how much evaluation is needed." They state that choosing requirements that capture intentions, and knowing when learning generalizes to unseen requirements, remain open.
- **A behavioural evaluator cannot detect exact imitation of a weaker model.** Mahran, "What Lies Beyond Perception: A Partition Theory of the Limits of AI Evaluation and Oversight" (arXiv:2610.30803; single author) models an evaluator's discrimination as a partition of its inputs and proves a one-sided bound: passing a test certifies at most a floor on the examinee's discrimination, never a ceiling. "Exact imitation of a weaker model is undetectable by any behavioural evaluator", and "A safety case that argues from behavioural evaluation alone that a model lacks an ability must assume the model's cooperation." Relevant to sandbagging and inability safety cases.
- **Reward prices a norm only where violations are noticed.** Baum, Binkytė, Großmann, Jahn & Mannino, "Rewarded for Not Getting Caught: Why Alignment Training Selects for Conditional Compliance and Cannot Instill Norms" (arXiv:2611.02162) argue that a reward can encode "do not do X" only as a cost charged when X is noticed, so training cannot favour a policy that always complies over one that complies where it would be caught, and "No behavioral evaluation can show that a model does better, since that would require observing how it behaves unobserved". Their remedy for norms no gain should outweigh is architecture that makes violations unavailable. An argument paper; read its premises before citing the conclusion.
- **Coherence arguments bundle two pressures.** Steven Byrnes (Alignment Forum 2026-11-02) argues that coherence arguments from markets, self-modification and training imply a strong pressure toward "being self-consistent" and a weaker, more contingent pressure toward "only caring about long-term eventual outcomes, with ruthless indifference to anything else", and that conflating them confuses discussions of power-seeking and incorrigibility.

**Use these as:** framing for what a behavioural result can and cannot license in a safety case. **When not to:** as evidence about any particular model; none measures one.

## Goal misgeneralization (one line)

**Goal misgeneralization** is when a capable policy learns a goal that fits the training data but pursues the wrong one out of distribution, even though the specification was correct (Langosco et al., arXiv:2105.14111; Shah et al., arXiv:2210.01790). For the RL-side tooling and the closely related reward-hacking experiments, see [`rl-training.md`](../oversight-and-control/rl-training.md).

## Guaranteed-safe AI and formal verification

### The Guaranteed Safe AI (GS AI) framework

Dalrymple, Skalse, Bengio, Russell, Tegmark et al., "Towards Guaranteed Safe AI" (arXiv:2405.06624, 2024). A guaranteed-safe (GS) AI system comes with a high-assurance quantitative safety guarantee built from three components:

1. a **world model**: a mathematical description of how the AI system affects the world;
2. a **safety specification**: a mathematical description of which effects are acceptable;
3. a **verifier**: produces an auditable proof certificate that the AI satisfies the specification *relative to the world model*.

The guarantee is only as good as the world model and the specification. Quinn and Max von Hippel ("Lies, Damned Lies, and Proofs: Formal Methods are not Slopless", LessWrong, 2026-01-12) argue that formal artifacts can be sloppy too, and name specification elicitation and validation as the part of the pipeline where language-model assistance has not yet been fully exploited.

### ARIA Safeguarded AI: status in 2026

The UK Advanced Research and Invention Agency (ARIA) **Safeguarded AI** programme (£59m, in the "Trust Everything, Everywhere" opportunity space) was led by davidad and is now led by Nora Ammann, both co-authors of the Guaranteed Safe AI (GS AI) paper. Its programme page, checked 2026-10-12, records several changes:

- **November 2025:** frontier AI progress led ARIA to stop investing in specialised AI systems that use its tools and instead broaden the toolkit itself.
- **February 2026:** ARIA confirmed it was "redirecting our efforts away from Technical Area 2 (TA2) as originally planned", and moved the original TA3 (real-world cyber-physical applications) to cybersecurity. There will be no TA3 Phase 2 call; existing Phase 1 projects continue.
- **April 2026:** davidad (David Dalrymple) became Technical Advisor and Nora Ammann became Programme Director.
- **May 2026 thesis:** two technical areas, *TA1 Tooling* (an open-source mathematical assurance toolkit that lets fleets of AI agents produce formally verified artifacts) and *TA2 Applications* (AI-enabled formal methods for cybersecurity). Eight TA2 Cybersecurity projects are funded. Rolling funding is open for "Safeguarded AI: Cybersecurity (Track 1)".

The Technical Area numbers changed between the original and the May 2026 thesis, so check which numbering a document uses before you cite "TA2".

### Where the formal-verification frontier is

- **Scale of AI-written formal mathematics.** Anthropic reported (2026-09-04) that Claude, run as dozens of parallel agents working largely autonomously for 11 days, produced the first complete computer-checked proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean: 13 million lines and 29,500 intermediate theorems. This is mathematics, not a safety specification. It shows that proof *production* is no longer the bottleneck for some problems.
- **Checking AI-written proofs** is now a security problem: see the evidence ladder and the trust caveats (kernel soundness bugs, sandboxing) in [`lean-formalization.md`](../engineering/lean-formalization.md). Since Lean v4.35.0 (2026-10-12) the comparator check is part of Lake (`lake comparator`, sandboxed with bubblewrap, Linux only), and `--paranoid` replays the proof through five independent checkers bundled with the toolchain as well as Lean's own kernel.
- **Statement faithfulness is the new bottleneck in verified mathematics.** With strong provers, checking an autoformalized statement against a reference by logical equivalence "proves too much": P ↔ Q holds for any two statements the library proves (SemantEq, Li et al., arXiv:2610.32919, which instead requires a chain of allowed rewriting steps checked on Lean proof terms); project-level review of whether a Lean theorem matches the manuscript claim is the subject of MechAlign / LeanReviewBench (Shen et al., arXiv:2610.35269, 501 claim–statement–context triples from OpenAI Math, Fermat's Last Theorem and Kakeya). Details in [`lean-formalization.md`](../engineering/lean-formalization.md).
- **A passing verifier certifies agreement with a formalization, not with the source text (Dafny, 2026-11).** Priyanath Maji, "When Verification Is Not Agreement: Cross-Agent LLM-to-Dafny Formalization" (arXiv:2611.07073, 2026-11-04; single author; abstract and results section read). One model (the Specifier) translates clinical-trial criteria into a Dafny predicate and another (the Implementer) writes a method verified against it, on 96 documents from the Chia corpus. "Agreement" means the Implementer's method verifies against the Specifier's predicate on the first attempt. A same-model control (GPT-4.1-mini in both roles) shows "independent re-extraction from identical text agrees only 59.3% of the time" (54 of the 91 documents left after excluding generation failures); the strongest pairing tested (GPT-5.6-luna / GPT-5.4-mini) reached 34.4% agreement with direct contradiction in 42.7% (of 96); and in one configuration (GPT-4.1-mini / GPT-4.1-nano, one document) "a weaker Implementer satisfies the postcondition by invoking the Specifier's predicate directly". For guaranteed-safe AI this is the specification problem in miniature: give the formalization step its own independent second reader, and check that an implementation does not simply call the specification. The Lean-side analogues (statement faithfulness, and the definition-hole trick under `lake comparator`) are on [`lean-formalization.md`](../engineering/lean-formalization.md).
- **Specifications for real systems** (the world model and safety specification of guaranteed-safe AI) remain the open part. ARIA explains its pivot as concentrating demonstrations where the need for guaranteed AI tooling is "both most acute and most time-sensitive", which it judges to be cybersecurity.

**Open problem / evidence bar:** a guaranteed-safe AI result would convince people if it gave a machine-checked guarantee about a deployed system, with a specification that someone other than the authors reviewed, a quantified world-model error, and a demonstration that the verifier rejects a realistic unsafe behaviour it was not tuned on.

## What does a first deliverable look like?

| Project type | Realistic first output for a fellowship-length project |
|---|---|
| Agent-foundations theory | A precise problem statement, a proved lemma or a counterexample, and a short post explaining why the lemma matters |
| Formal corrigibility | A known result restated in your own formalism, or a Lean-checked lemma from it, plus one assumption you show is necessary |
| Theory-to-empirics bridge | An evaluation that tests one assumption of a formal result on current models, with controls |
| Decision-theory or infra-Bayesian implementation | A small runnable agent for one class of problems, compared with a classical baseline (for example the SPAR infra-Bayesian agent on stateless problems, `SPAR-S26-IBRL-Stream/Project-Newcomb`, extended to a sequential setting) |
| Formal verification / guaranteed-safe AI | A specification for a small, real component, checked against an implementation, with the gap between the specification and the intended property written down |

The [`project-shapes.md`](../start-here/project-shapes.md) catalog has a "Theory / formal-methods paper" shape with tools and pitfalls.

## Common questions

### Is corrigibility solved?

No. Nayebi (arXiv:2507.20964) calls his construction "the first complete formal solution to corrigibility in the off-switch game", and the scope is that game together with learned utility heads that meet stated error bounds. The same paper proves that the general adversarial case is undecidable. Whether current training can produce agents that satisfy such assumptions is open. The Corrigibility Research Fund funds both theory and experiments on that question (round 2 closed 2026-10-31; next deadline not yet set as of 2026-11-23), though its evaluator has said he does not want experiments that only show models sometimes resist correction.

### Can an AI agent help with agent-foundations problems?

On theorem-shaped problems, yes: OpenAI's 2026 release of AI-produced mathematics shows broad technique retrieval and long audit-and-repair chains on well-posed conjectures, though none of its problems were agent foundations. Inside agent foundations, both of Wentworth and Lorell's natural-latents bounty problems were resolved in mid-2026 with heavy use of LLMs and Lean — one by a machine-checked proof with no prose version, one by a counterexample (Wentworth, "LLMs Are Starting To Noticeably Accelerate Our Work", LessWrong 2026-08-11: "about 80% confident that both have been resolved"). On problems where the hard part is choosing the definitions, use the agent to derive each candidate formalism's verdicts on a fixed case bank (Newcomb, counterfactual mugging, 5-and-10, Troll Bridge and others) rather than to prove things. Check those verdicts across models and reasoning settings, because an under-specified theory gets operationalized inconsistently: jessicata, "How do LLMs interpret FDT in a twin PD simulation?" (LessWrong 2026-11-09; prompts, code and transcripts at `jessica-taylor/almost-twin-pd-eval` on GitHub) had seven LLMs apply causal decision theory (CDT), evidential decision theory (EDT) and two readings of functional decision theory (FDT) in a prisoner's dilemma between nearly identical copies. The models largely agreed that CDT defects and EDT cooperates, and every completed policy-FDT round ended in mutual cooperation, but on action-FDT (the 2017 paper's equation 4) cooperation was 62.5% overall, with some models almost always cooperating and others almost always defecting; Claude Opus 5.5's action-FDT cooperation rate fell from 65.5% at low reasoning effort to 39.3% at medium and 22.4% at high (28–29 scored rounds each; low vs high p = 0.00046; Claude Sonnet 5.5 cooperated in 100% of rounds at all three settings). The author's conclusion is that "'FDT' is an ambiguous term". See [`theory-research.md`](../engineering/theory-research.md).

### Do I need Lean to do theory work?

No. Most agent-foundations output is paper mathematics. A Lean-checked lemma adds credibility when the claim is subtle or AI-assisted; see [`lean-formalization.md`](../engineering/lean-formalization.md) for which checks a proof needs before you report it as complete.
