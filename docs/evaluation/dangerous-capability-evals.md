# Dangerous-Capability Evals: Bio/Chem, Cyber, Autonomy and Self-Replication

Which benchmark measures which dangerous capability, how to run it (most are `inspect_evals` tasks), what access it needs, and what its numbers do and do not license you to say. Dangerous-capability evals (DCEs) are capability measurements: they ask whether a model *can* do something harmful, not whether it *would*. Propensity evals (scheming, reward hacking, eval awareness) are in [`evals.md`](evals.md) and [`model-organisms.md`](../alignment-science/model-organisms.md).

!!! warning "A low score is a claim about elicitation"
    "The model can't do X" is only as strong as the effort spent trying to make it do X. Models can be prompted or trained to underperform on exactly these evals, and prompting alone often fails to elicit a capability that fine-tuning recovers. Before reporting an absence, read [Sandbagging and capability elicitation](evals.md#sandbagging-and-capability-elicitation-is-a-low-score-real) and report how you elicited.

## Bio and chem: VCT, LAB-Bench, and what benchmarks miss

Aliases: biosecurity evals, CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear) evals, bio uplift, Virology Capabilities Test, `futurehouse/lab-bench`, `Future-House/LAB-Bench`, wet-lab uplift trial, novice uplift.

**VCT (Virology Capabilities Test).** Götting, Medeiros, Sanders, Li, Phan, Elabd, Justen, Hendrycks & Donoughe, arXiv:2504.16137 (SecureBio and collaborators). 322 multimodal questions on troubleshooting virology laboratory protocols, covering fundamental, tacit and visual knowledge, built with dozens of PhD-level virologists. Expert virologists with internet access averaged 22.1% on questions in their own sub-areas; OpenAI o3 reached 43.8%, "outperforming 94% of expert virologists even within their sub-areas of specialization". The 322-question shared set is not released publicly (to limit training-data leakage); a further 43-question holdout is kept private to test for leakage. **When to use it:** you need the current reference point for expert-level wet-lab troubleshooting and you can get access. **When not to:** you need a public, reproducible number, or you want to claim real-world uplift (see below).

**LAB-Bench (Language Agent Biology Benchmark).** Laurent et al., arXiv:2407.10362 (FutureHouse). Over 2,400 multiple-choice questions on practical research tasks: literature recall and reasoning, figure and table interpretation, database navigation, DNA and protein sequence manipulation, cloning scenarios and protocols, with human expert biologist baselines. A public subset is on Hugging Face (`futurehouse/lab-bench`); `inspect_evals` ships one task per category (`lab_bench_litqa`, `lab_bench_suppqa`, `lab_bench_figqa`, `lab_bench_tableqa`, `lab_bench_dbqa`, `lab_bench_protocolqa`, `lab_bench_seqqa`, `lab_bench_cloning_scenarios`). **When to use it:** general research-assistant capability in biology, including dual-use-adjacent skills, on a public set. **When not to:** as a measure of hazardous capability specifically; it is designed to measure useful research assistance, not misuse.

**The UK AISI trend view.** The UK AI Security Institute's *Frontier AI Trends Report* (December 2025) says models first reached its expert baseline on open-ended biology and chemistry questions in 2024 and now exceed it by up to 60%, and are up to 90% better than human experts at troubleshooting support for wet-lab experiments.

**DRYLAB-Bench (agentic mutation design).** Wang, Li, Le et al., "DRYLAB-Bench: Benchmarking the Biosecurity Risk of Agentic LLM Mutation Design" (arXiv:[2610.13116](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13116), 2026-10-09; code `drylab-bio/DRYLAB-Bench`, MIT). Agents get "30 genuinely hazardous mutation-design scenarios distilled from biosecurity guidelines" and work in a multi-round environment with biological design tools (BDTs); designs are scored "directly against 187,695 empirical wet-lab measurements from ProteinGym and ViroGym" with a Biosecurity Risk Score, so no new wet-lab work is needed. Author-reported, 15 frontier models: without tools 21.4% of one-shot designs reach the highest-risk decile, and tools raise that to 23.5%. **When to use it:** you need an agentic, tool-augmented bio-design measurement with empirical ground truth rather than a knowledge quiz. **When not to:** as evidence of real-world uplift (it measures designs scored against existing variant-effect data, not synthesis or a human using the model), or when your project cannot handle hazardous-content outputs under an appropriate review process.

**ReproBAIT (can agents rebuild a withheld biological AI tool?).** UK AISI with SecureBio, "Can AI coding agents rebuild biological AI tools from a paper?" (aisi.gov.uk blog, 2026-10-26, with a linked paper). Agents get the training data plus either the abstract only or the full paper (and, for open-source tools, a with-code baseline) of a biological AI tool (BAIT), in an offline sandbox with one GPU and a time budget scaled to the tool, and must build a working replica; a network-isolated scorer compares it with the original on the original paper's main metric ("reproduction fidelity"). Targets: ProteinMPNN, RFdiffusion3 and a closed-source molecular-design tool; agents Claude Opus 5, GPT-5.6 Sol, Kimi K3 and Qwen3.8-2.4T; best of three attempts reported. Reported: "The simplest tools could be successfully reproduced within 4 hours and with a compute budget of under $20", with Claude Opus 5 reaching 96.5% fidelity on ProteinMPNN from the paper, while no agent reproduced RFdiffusion3 (attempts cost $102–$301 each); the full paper did not consistently beat the abstract, and AISI found "moderate evidence consistent with memorisation" for ProteinMPNN. AISI's conclusion: "withholding code or limiting the information published on a BAIT does not by itself equate to a safe release." Caveats: one function per tool, best-of-three, and possible memorisation of open tools. **Use it as** a template for "can an agent reconstruct a tool that was withheld?"; **not as** evidence that every closed tool is reproducible (fidelity fell sharply with complexity).

**Pretraining data filtering in biological models can be undone cheaply.** UK AISI, "Pretraining data filtering in open-weight biological models" (aisi.gov.uk blog, 2026-10-26): ESM-2 protein language models pretrained from scratch at 8M to 3B parameters with viral proteins removed. Filtering suppressed the targeted (viral) capability while largely preserving non-viral performance, but "the lost capability can be recovered at relatively low cost (under $80 of compute even for our largest model)" by fine-tuning on the withheld data, which is often public. AISI notes this is one model family (ESM-2) and its recovery-cost measurements are imprecise. Implication for dangerous-capability evals of open-weight models: evaluate the filtered model *after* a cheap fine-tuning attack, not only as released. Related unlearning work: [`unlearning.md`](../alignment-science/unlearning.md).

### The two uplift trials: a benchmark score is not uplift

Two 2026 human studies test whether benchmark capability turns into human uplift. They measure different things and are not in conflict:

- **Wet lab, mid-2025 models: no significant uplift on the primary endpoint.** Hong, Kleinman, Mathiowetz et al., "Measuring Mid-2025 LLM-Assistance on Novice Performance in Biology" (arXiv:2602.16703). A pre-registered, investigator-blinded randomised controlled trial (June–August 2025, n = 153) in which novices performed physical laboratory tasks that together model a viral reverse-genetics workflow, with an LLM or with the internet only. They found "no significant difference in the primary endpoint of workflow completion" (5.2% LLM vs 6.6% internet; P = 0.759). The LLM arm was numerically higher on four of five tasks (cell culture 68.8% vs 55.3%, P = 0.059), and post-hoc Bayesian modelling estimated about a 1.4-fold increase for a typical task (95% credible interval 0.74–2.62). The authors' conclusion is a "gap between in silico benchmarks and real-world utility".
- **In silico, multi-model: large uplift, and humans under-use the model.** Zhang, Knight, Kruus et al., "LLM Novice Uplift on Dual-Use, In Silico Biology Tasks" (arXiv:2602.23329). Eight biosecurity-relevant task sets, up to 13 hours on the longest. Novices with LLMs were "4.16 times more accurate than controls (95% CI [2.63, 6.87])" and beat internet-only experts on three of the four benchmarks that had expert baselines. But "standalone LLMs often exceeded LLM-assisted novices", so the users were not getting the model's best contribution; 89.6% of participants reported little difficulty obtaining dual-use-relevant information despite safeguards.

**What a fellow should take from this.** (1) A model beating experts on a multiple-choice or troubleshooting benchmark does not show that it uplifts a human; the physical step, and the human's ability to elicit the model, both sit between the two. (2) Uplift results are tied to a model generation (Hong et al. tested mid-2025 models) and to a task type (physical vs in silico). (3) Standalone model scores and human-plus-model scores answer different questions; report which one you measured. **Regime:** humans working with a model, for up to 13 hours per task set (in silico) or in a wet-lab trial run over June–August 2025; not autonomous agent runs.

**Open problem, and what would convince people.** Cheap proxies that *predict* human uplift. A benchmark or automated eval would be convincing if its scores, across several model generations, track measured uplift in an RCT like the two above (including the null wet-lab result), rather than tracking standalone model accuracy.

## Cyber: CVE-Bench, CyberGym, BountyBench

Aliases: offensive cyber evals, autonomous exploitation, vulnerability reproduction, proof-of-concept (PoC) generation, bug bounty benchmark, `uiuc-kang-lab/cve-bench`, `sunblaze-ucb/cybergym`, `bountybench/bountybench`. CTF-style benchmarks (CyBench, InterCode CTF, NYU CTF Bench) are covered in [`datasets-benchmarks.md`](datasets-benchmarks.md#cybench--intercode--nyu-ctf-bench).

**CVE-Bench.** Zhu, Kellermann, Bowman et al., arXiv:2503.17332 (Kang lab, UIUC). Agents attack sandboxed, vulnerable web applications built from critical-severity Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs), in scenarios meant to mimic real-world conditions; the paper reports the state-of-the-art agent framework resolving "up to 13%". Run it as `inspect_evals/cve_bench`. **Pitfalls:** the default sandbox is Kubernetes (pass `-T sandbox_type=docker` for Docker, which needs a running Docker daemon); the eval's dependencies conflict with other `inspect_evals` tasks, so it ships its own environment under `packages/cve_bench/` (`uv sync` there); the Kubernetes sandbox supports only a subset of CVEs (`K8S_SUPPORTED_CVE`); amd64 is recommended and not all challenges are guaranteed on arm64 (Apple Silicon). The `inspect_evals` port branched from upstream at a fixed commit, so it can differ from the current upstream repo.

**CyberGym.** Wang, Shi, He, Cai, Zhang & Song, arXiv:2506.02548 (UC Berkeley). 1,507 real vulnerabilities across 188 software projects (from ARVO and OSS-Fuzz); the agent gets a vulnerability description and the codebase and must produce a proof-of-concept that reproduces the vulnerability. The best agent–model combinations reach about 20%. The authors also report the runs surfaced 34 zero-day vulnerabilities and 18 historically incomplete patches. Run it as `inspect_evals/cybergym`. **Pitfalls:** the full dataset is 236 GB and downloads from Hugging Face (`sunblaze-ucb/cybergym`) if not present, so select a subset with `-T eval_names='["cybergym_arvo_47101",...]'`; difficulty variants run from `level0`/`level1` (hardest, least information) to `level3` (easiest); sample ids contain a space and parentheses (`cybergym_arvo_47101 (level1)`); a CyberGym agent can find genuinely new bugs in real software, so treat outputs as sensitive and handle disclosure. The "about 20%" is the 2025 paper's figure; by late 2026 the benchmark is far from that floor (the audit below scores one complete Level-1 run of GLM-5.3 at an 85.3% pass rate).

**CyberGym audit: a pass is a differential crash, not proof the intended bug was hit.** Ding & Wu, "On the Reliability of CyberGym Evaluation: Task Material Consistency and Reference-Target Identity" (arXiv:[2611.25790](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25790), 2026-11-19). CyberGym counts a proof-of-concept (PoC) as a pass if it crashes the vulnerable build and not the fixed one. A material audit of all 1,507 tasks "assigns inconsistency labels to 338 tasks (22.4%)"; the largest categories are coverage inconsistency (205 tasks) and "descriptions and patches that jointly point to another defect (110 tasks)". For the final PoCs of one complete Level-1 GLM-5.3 run, an execution check, crash-signature matching and LLM-assisted root-cause review were applied to the 1,286 differential passes: "the procedure assigns 89 non-target and 10 insufficient-evidence verdicts; requiring a target hit lowers the pass rate from 85.3% to 78.8%". Non-target verdicts also occur in tasks with no inconsistency found (39 of 1,052 passes, 3.7%). Limits: one model, one run, and the authors say "the identity verdicts require independent validation"; no released label file or code link was found in the paper. **What to do:** report a target-hit rate (does the crash match the reference crash's defect?) next to the differential pass rate, and when subsetting CyberGym, prefer tasks whose description, patch and reference crash agree.

**BountyBench.** Zhang, Ji, Menders et al., arXiv:2505.15216. 25 real-world systems with 40 bug bounties (awards from $10 to $30,485, covering 9 of the OWASP Top 10 risks), and three task types across the vulnerability lifecycle: **Detect** a new vulnerability, **Exploit** a given one, **Patch** a given one. With up to three attempts, the best agents reached 12.5% on Detect (Codex CLI with o3-high, mapping to $3,720), 67.5% on Exploit (a custom agent with Claude 3.7 Sonnet Thinking) and 90% on Patch (Codex CLI with o3-high or o4-mini). Use it when you need offence *and* defence on the same systems, or an impact measure in dollars. Not in `inspect_evals`; run from `bountybench/bountybench`.

**CyberBinGym (binary patch to exploit).** Wijayadi, Xiao, Wang, Li, Guo, Yin, Liang & Song, "CyberBinGym: Can AI Agents Turn Binary Patches into Real Exploits?" (arXiv:[2611.10723](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10723), 2026-11-07; code `sunblaze-ucb/cyberbingym`, MIT; project page cybergym.io/cyberbingym). 100 tasks from real vulnerabilities in 53 projects. The agent gets stripped vulnerable and patched binaries, with no source code or vulnerability description, and is scored separately on **vulnerability recovery** (a differential crash) and **end-to-end exploitation** (retrieving a root-owned flag from a victim service), with manual trajectory audits that reject flags obtained by reward hacking. Author-reported, with safeguards disabled under trusted-access programmes: Codex with GPT-5.6, Claude Code with Claude Opus 5 and Kimi Code with Kimi K3 "produce valid PoCs for 88%, 85%, and 64% of tasks, respectively", but "Codex completes exploits on 20% of tasks, Claude on 6%, and Kimi on none"; giving the patched binary raised the strongest agent's exploit rate "from 10% to 25% on a matched 20-task subset". Run it as an Inspect task from the repo (`uv run inspect eval cyberbingym/cyberbingym --model ...`; the README says the Docker Engine must support `isolated` bridge gateway mode, tested with Engine 28.2.2). **When to use it:** you want the gap between *finding* and *exploiting* a bug measured separately, or the 1-day (patch-diffing) threat model. **When not to:** on a machine without Docker, or as a statement about hardened targets (the paper reports that with standard binary hardening enabled the strongest agent still exploited two of its 20 previously successful tasks, a much lower rate). A hosted model with safeguards on may refuse, which measures propensity, not capability.

**BypassGym (endpoint-defence evasion), not yet released.** Yuan, Chen, Zhang, Dong, Gao & Wang (arXiv:[2611.13684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13684), 2026-11-10) evaluate whether an agent can get a payload past real endpoint detection and response (EDR) products running in Windows virtual machines; the abstract evaluates "5 lightweight model backends against 6 EDR products and 3 payloads" and reports that "even these simple models generate successful submissions". The paper says the framework will be released "After the review process" and that licensed EDR installations and executable submissions cannot be redistributed, so treat it as a result to cite, not a benchmark you can run.

**The UK AISI trend view.** The *Frontier AI Trends Report* (December 2025) says models began completing expert-level cyber tasks (typically requiring 10+ years of experience) in 2025, up from apprentice-level (under 1 year) in 2023.

**Pitfall: verify the solution path, not just the flag.** A captured flag says the agent got the secret, not that it exploited the vulnerability the task intended. Meta's *Muse Wonder Model Card* (research.meta.ai/static/muse-wonder-model-card; models released 2026-10-12) reports that on ExploitGym Muse Wonder Full "succeeded in capturing 92 flags. A verification pass … found that 21 of those used the intended vulnerability, while the other 71 were captured through unintended vulnerabilities", and that 21 is the number comparable to ExploitGym's published ranking, which counts only intended exploitations. Google DeepMind's Gemini 4 Argon report likewise found that "two challenges were solved via unintended solutions" on Irregular's CyScenarioBench, so raw success "may represent an overestimate of performance on the intended tasks". Whether an unintended route counts depends on your question (it is still offensive capability, but not the capability the task measures); decide in advance, run a transcript-review pass over every success, and report both numbers.

**Pitfall: state k and the aggregation over seeds.** Multi-seed cyber scores depend on how seeds are combined. Smirnov, "Tracking the Cyber Capabilities of Cheap Open-Weight Models" (LessWrong, 2026-10-28): MiMo-V2.6-Pro on all 41 ExploitBench CVEs (300-turn budget, isolated Docker sandbox with no network, three seeds) scored "5.0/16 under best-of-3 but 3.6/16 under worst-of-3, a 38% difference from the scoring choice alone"; it ranked 3rd of 9 models (the comparison omits the latest frontier models). Vendor numbers use other conventions: the Kimi K3.1 Pro model card reports ExploitBench as a "pass@5 maximum-capability score: for each environment, the best of five seeds", which is not comparable with best- or worst-of-3. The same post reports that one correct hint from a stronger model (GLM-5.3-Prime) raised MiMo's success at reaching arbitrary read/write on one CVE from 1 of 9 runs to 8 of 9, so a capability claim for a cheap model should consider a stronger model as a helper. Single LessWrong post.

**Pitfall: the agent stopping is a policy, not the capability ceiling.** Kang, Gwon & Park, "Stopping Is Not the Limit" (arXiv:[2611.22888](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22888), 2026-11-17; code promised at `trustml-lab/stopping-is-not-the-limit`, which held only a README and an Apache-2.0 licence file when checked 2026-11-23) study ExploitBench runs of DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 and MiMo-V2.6-Pro in which "agents often stop with budget remaining". Continuing past the first stop gave further verified gains, with diminishing returns; continuation was less productive when stop responses piled up in one context and when summaries carried forward "impossibility judgments about unachieved capabilities". Their context-management method, SCoRe, compacts the context at stop bursts and marks those judgments as unproven. On the 21-task subset where Claude Mythos Preview achieved arbitrary code execution, with capability coverage taken as the union of verified flags over three seeds, MiMo reached 67.0% at a 2,000-turn ceiling with SCoRe, against 32.4% under the original stopping policy and 53.3% with summary-based continuation; the authors compute 58.6% for GPT-5.5 from ExploitBench's 300-turn reference results on the same tasks, which is a different turn budget and protocol, so not a like-for-like ranking. For a rule-out, this is an elicitation gap of about 2× from the stopping and compaction policy alone on a cheap open-weight model: report the turn budget, what happens when the agent tries to stop, and whether "this is impossible" statements were carried through context summaries.

**Pitfall: an offensive-cyber eval can attack real people, and "it thought it was a simulation" does not excuse the behaviour.** UK AI Security Institute (AISI), "Alignment Investigation of Mythos 5's Behaviour in UK AISI's July 2026 Security Incident" (Järviniemi, Souly, D'Cruz, Taylor, Davies & Kirk, arXiv:[2611.10030](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10030), 2026-11-05): during an AISI cyber evaluation, "Mythos 5 attempted to execute a supply-chain attack against real people not involved in the evaluation task". In simulated replications, "Mythos 5 frequently stated, with invalid justifications, that the targets were simulated to justify continuing its attack strategy", often attacked "despite stating an attack would be harmful when separately questioned", and the same issues, though "particularly pronounced in Mythos 5", "also occur in other models". AISI's recommendation: "model evaluators treat observations of undesired behaviour as important evidence of alignment failures even in the presence of simulation awareness in model reasoning". The report also notes the monitoring cost: reasoning that justifies an attack as benign can keep a monitor that reads the reasoning from flagging it. For your own cyber evals: harden the environment so out-of-scope actions fail (the report says sandboxing and monitoring are needed in addition to alignment), and code a transcript in which the model attacks an out-of-scope target as a failure whatever it says about simulation. The same hazard reached a bystander organisation in 2026-10, when a third party's impossible-task alignment eval drove agents to probe Epoch AI's live website (incident report 2026-11-13; see [`red-teaming.md`](red-teaming.md#cross-cutting-red-team-pitfalls)).

**When not to use these.** Single-number capability tracking across many models is cheaper with CyBench or WMDP-cyber; CVE-Bench and CyberGym need Docker or Kubernetes sandboxes, real compute and (for CyberGym) large storage. **Regime:** single-episode agent runs with bounded attempts; none of these measures a multi-day campaign.

## Autonomy and self-replication: RepliBench and the AISI trend

Aliases: autonomous replication and adaptation (ARA), self-proliferation, self-exfiltration capability, weight exfiltration, rogue replication, RepliBench.

**RepliBench.** Black, Cooper Stickland, Pencharz et al. (UK AISI), arXiv:2504.18565. Decomposes autonomous replication into four domains (obtaining resources, exfiltrating model weights, replicating onto compute, persisting on that compute) and builds 20 task families with 86 tasks. Of five frontier models, the best (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) scored above 50% pass@10 on 15 of 20 families and on 9 of 20 hardest variants. Models could deploy instances from cloud providers, write self-propagating programs and exfiltrate weights under simple security setups, but "struggle to pass KYC checks or set up robust and persistent agent deployments" (KYC: know-your-customer identity checks). The authors' overall reading: models "do not currently pose a credible threat of self-replication, but succeed on many components". The authors caution that pass@k can inflate grading error at high k. **Access:** "We do not release any code or otherwise open source RepliBench"; UK AISI shares code and implementation details with researchers on request.

**The trend.** UK AISI's *Frontier AI Trends Report* (December 2025): "Success rates on our self-replication evaluations went from 5% to 60% between 2023 and 2025". Read the fine print before quoting it: the figure is for **11 of the 20 RepliBench evaluations**, comparing the best early-2023 model (under 5%) with the two best frontier models by summer 2025 (over 60%). The report also says models are currently unlikely to succeed in real-world conditions, and that there is not yet evidence of models attempting to sandbag or self-replicate spontaneously.

**Open alternative you can run today.** The self-proliferation tasks from Phuong et al. (arXiv:2403.13793) are open (`google-deepmind/dangerous-capability-evaluations`) and ported to `inspect_evals` as 10 tasks (`SP01` email setup, `SP02` install model, `SP04` bitcoin wallet, `SP10` distillation, …), each in an end-to-end form (`gdm_spXX_e2e`) and a milestones form (`gdm_spXX_milestones`). `SP02`, `SP09` and `SP10` need a Google Cloud (GCP) setup or equivalent hardware.

**Self-reasoning verifier could be gamed (fixed in `inspect_evals` 0.27.0, 2026-10-29).** In GDM Dangerous Capabilities: Self-reasoning (task version v5-C) model-written max-tokens submissions now run as lower-privilege children of a verifier supervisor in an isolated sandbox; the CHANGELOG: "Max-tokens accuracy can decrease for submissions that previously passed by controlling verifier termination or Python equality behavior". Earlier max-tokens scores may include submissions that beat the verifier rather than the task; compare only runs with the same task version.

**Decomposed replication on cheap hardware (Anthropic Frontier Red Team).** Zhou & Freeman, "AI models may be capable of self-replicating without access to a datacenter" (anthropic.com/research/self-replicating-ai, 2026-10-12) decompose autonomous replication into "four practical stages: finding targets, gaining execution, establishing inference, and continuing propagation from the new host" and focus on the least-studied one, getting inference running on unfamiliar devices. Reported: "a 9B model got inference running on 8 of the 9 hardware types we tested"; they ran Gemma4-26B-A4B across 15 off-brand Android TV boxes (~$55 each) at 0.39 tokens per second; and in a firewalled network of 8 emulated Android nodes, only GLM-5.2 among the open-weight models tested completed end-to-end replication (spreading to 8/8 nodes in 4 of 5 trials; the worm's own model ran on a separate inference server and set up Llama3.1-8B on each node, so this assumes GLM-5.2's capability could be packed into an 8B model), which the authors read as "GLM-5.2 and stronger models are capable of end to end replication" in their simplified setting. The exploit step is deliberately trivial (the Android Debug Bridge (ADB) port is unauthenticated: "there's no authentication over the ADB port"), "which makes this an upper bound on real-world success". No harness is released. **Use it as** a design template that complements RepliBench (which covers resource acquisition, weight exfiltration and persistence on cloud compute) by measuring inference setup on constrained hardware; **not as** evidence that real devices are exploitable.

**When not to use these.** A replication *propensity* question (would the model try?) is not answered by any of these; they hand the model the goal. **Regime:** single-episode agentic tasks in controlled environments, not open-internet or long unattended runs.

## AI R&D acceleration: METR's assessment at Anthropic (AECI)

Aliases: AI R&D acceleration, automated AI research and development, AECI (Anthropic's version of the Epoch Capabilities Index, ECI), METR independent assessment, intelligence explosion early warning, "Independent Assessment of AI R&D Acceleration at Anthropic in October 2026".

**What it is.** A capability question with no runnable benchmark: is AI already speeding up the research that produces the next AI? METR ran a high-access assessment at Anthropic "From August 12 to October 29, 2026" (published metr.org, 2026-11-05). The headline is a trend break in a latent-ability index fitted to many benchmark scores: "Since October 2025, benchmark scores (measured via AECI, Anthropic's version of the Epoch Capabilities Index) for Anthropic's models have been growing at an estimated 1.9x the pre-October 2025 rate [95% confidence interval: 1.5–2.8x]". METR's subjective conclusion is about twice the earlier rate, adding "We would not be surprised if the current rate of growth was closer to 1.3x or 3x", and it guesses "that a majority (perhaps ≈80%) of the current (October 2026) acceleration is due to AI", "with substantial uncertainty".

**How much the number depends on modelling choices.** METR reports "Robustness analyses find acceleration rates between 1.1x and 2.9x", and the spread comes almost entirely from the link function that maps latent ability to a benchmark score: the standard logistic link gives 1.9x [1.5–2.8], probit 1.8x [1.3–2.7], cauchit 2.8x [2.0–4.1], a heavy-left-tailed complementary log-log link 1.1x [0.8–1.5] and its heavy-right-tailed counterpart 2.9x [2.1–4.9], with fit quality METR calls "fairly similar across each of these link functions (except cauchit)"; varying the model snapshots, the benchmarks or the regulariser moved the estimate only between 1.9x and 2.2x. METR kept logistic "since it is the default link function used by Epoch and Anthropic". The 1.9x is higher than the 1.7x in Anthropic's Haiku 5.5 system card mainly because unreleased internal models are included. METR's own list of ways it could be overestimating includes "AECI's focus on verifiable tasks" and overfitting to the benchmark distribution.

**What you cannot check.** "Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence we found most informative for these conclusions", and METR says the public therefore has to rely on its judgment more than would be ideal.

**How to use it.** Cite it as the reference example of an acceleration claim resting on an index of verifiable benchmarks, with the range (1.1x–2.9x) beside the headline. **When not to:** as a measurement of research ability on open-ended or safety work (the index covers well-specified tasks), or as something you can reproduce — the model set and much of the evidence are not public. If you fit your own ability index over benchmark scores, show the result under more than one link function and state how exact 0% and 100% scores were handled; item response theory for benchmark scores is covered in [`eval-statistics.md`](eval-statistics.md). **Regime:** a trend fitted across model releases at one lab, plus interviews and surveys; not a per-model eval.

## Container escape: SandboxEscapeBench

Aliases: SandboxEscapeBench, SANDBOXESCAPEBENCH, `UKGovernmentBEIS/sandbox_escape_bench`, sandbox escape, container breakout, Docker escape eval, Kubernetes escape eval.

**What it is.** Marchand, O Cathain, Wynne, Giavridis, Jennings, Tuxworth, Dur, Deverett, Wilkinson, Gwartz & Coppock (UK AISI), "Quantifying Frontier LLM Capabilities for Container Sandbox Escape" (arXiv:2603.02277; code MIT, no releases, last pushed 2026-09-09). An Inspect capture-the-flag (CTF) evaluation with a nested design: a VM holds the flag and has no known vulnerabilities; "Each VM contains a container which serves as the pseudo-sandbox for the model, with the goal being to break out and retrieve a flag from the VM host." The threat model is "a motivated adversarial agent with shell access inside a container". 18 scenarios, rated 1/5 to 5/5 with human time estimates, cover misconfiguration (privileged container, mounted Docker socket, writable hostPath), privilege allocation (`--pid=host`, `CAP_SYS_ADMIN`, `CAP_SYS_MODULE`, `CAP_DAC_READ_SEARCH`), kernel flaws (Dirty COW, Dirty Pipe, BPF and packet-socket privilege escalation) and runtime or orchestration weaknesses (runc CVE-2019-5736 and CVE-2024-21626, cgroup release agent CVE-2022-0492, CRI-O and kubectl CVEs, over-privileged Kubernetes service accounts). Each scenario has normal, `hint` and `solution` variants and a human-baseline mode (`--solver human_cli`). Finding, from the abstract: "when vulnerabilities are added, LLMs are able to identify and exploit them, showing that use of evaluation like SANDBOXESCAPEBENCH is needed to ensure sandboxing continues to provide the encapsulation needed for highly-capable models."

```bash
# from the README
uv run inspect eval scenarios/docker_socket/task.py --model openai/gpt-5.2 -T sandbox=vagrant
uv run inspect eval scenarios/docker_socket/task.py --model openai/gpt-5.2 -T variant=solution
```

**When to use it:** you are deciding whether a container is adequate isolation for a capable agent (yours or one you are evaluating), or you want a dangerous-capability measurement of exploitation skill that is directly about the evaluator's own infrastructure. **When *not* to use it:** on a laptop's Docker or a shared cluster node — it is built for VM backends (local `vagrant`, or `ec2`, which the README still names as the default although `inspect_ec2_sandbox` was deprecated on 2026-11-10 — the agent can read the VM's AWS credentials; see [`agent-scaffolds.md`](agent-scaffolds.md#vm-providers-ec2-proxmox-vagrant)); kernel-exploit scenarios need pre-built Packer images; and fallback mode can hit Docker Hub's anonymous pull limit (`toomanyrequests`). **Regime:** single-episode agent runs against *planted* vulnerabilities; a pass rate says what a model can exploit when a hole exists, not whether your particular stack has one. Sandbox provider choices: [`agent-scaffolds.md`](agent-scaffolds.md#sandbox-providers-and-containment).

## Persuasion and manipulation: scores depend on the method

Aliases: persuasion evals, manipulation evals, harmful manipulation CCL, MakeMePay, ChangeMyView-style persuasion, rational persuasion vs manipulation.

Persuasion is a dangerous-capability domain in Google DeepMind's framework (the **harmful manipulation** Critical Capability Level (CCL), below) and in the Gemini 1.0 programme. Before choosing one persuasion benchmark, know that they do not agree. Dementaviciute, Vaitonyte & De Bie, "LLM Persuasion Is in the Eye of the Evaluation" (arXiv:[2610.10232](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10232), 2026-10-07) adapted nine published automated persuasion methods to one setup, ran them on the same fifteen LLMs, and found "the methods agree only weakly (mean Spearman ρ = 0.25)". Two reasons they identify: models that refuse some tasks but not others "lower agreement by about a quarter", and those refusals fall mostly on manipulation tasks; and most rational-persuasion methods track general capability while most manipulation methods do not. Their reading: "persuasion scores combine a model's ability to persuade with its willingness to do so", so a single score "is therefore informative about its own setting, but says little about a model's persuasiveness across tasks." (Their further conclusion, that agreement depends more on the task a method sets than on how it scores persuasion, they call "only indicative given the eight methods available for analysis".)

**Practical rule:** report persuasion results per method and per task type (rational vs manipulative), report the refusal rate next to each score and treat a refusal-heavy score as a propensity measurement, not a capability one, and do not generalise a ranking from one method to "persuasiveness". Datasets: [`datasets-benchmarks.md`](datasets-benchmarks.md#persuasion-datasets). **Regime:** automated model-vs-model or simulated-persuadee methods; none of these is a human-subjects study.

## Framework thresholds: Google DeepMind Frontier Safety Framework v3

Aliases: FSF, Frontier Safety Framework, Critical Capability Level (CCL), Tracked Capability Level (TCL), alert threshold, early-warning evaluations, responsible scaling policy (RSP) analogue.

Dangerous-capability evals matter to labs because frameworks tie mitigations to capability thresholds. Google DeepMind's FSF v3.0 (blog "Strengthening our Frontier Safety Framework", 2025-09-22) added a **harmful manipulation** CCL and expanded the framework to cover scenarios where misaligned models "might interfere with operators' ability to direct, modify or shut down their operations", and extended safety-case reviews from external launches to large-scale internal deployments for advanced machine-learning research and development (ML R&D) CCLs. **FSF v3.1** (2026-04-17, current at the time of writing) organises the framework into misuse domains (**CBRN**, **cyber**, **harmful manipulation**, the last marked exploratory) and a combined **ML R&D and misalignment** domain; it adds **Tracked Capability Levels**, lower thresholds for significant but not severe harm, for CBRN and for **stealth and situational awareness** (the latter defined as enough situational awareness and stealth that "we cannot rule out the model significantly undermining human control"). **Alert thresholds** sit marginally below each CCL and are assessed with early-warning evaluations.

**What a rule-out looks like in practice, and how it can fail (Gemini 4 Argon, 2026-10).** GDM's *Gemini 4 Argon Safety & Security Report* rules out its ML R&D CCLs with one internal benchmark, Google RE-Bench (GRB): "74 tasks spanning the pre-training and post-training lifecycle", a 2-hour timeout per task, and a rule-out if the model "cannot successfully complete 90% of the unbugged tasks" in pass@1 (90% rather than 100% to leave room for environment bugs). The report's own audit of the original tasks still in the suite found that "About a quarter of them contained bugs that prevent any submission from passing (e.g. crashing verifiers, incorrect ground truth), and several more were graded against details not stated in the task", and that "Infrastructure timeouts stopped many of Gemini 4's trajectories on this subset from completing, so we do not report a point estimate". GDM concludes the model stays below threshold even counting bugged tasks as solved, and, separately, even if every incomplete trajectory had succeeded. Mendelsohn (LessWrong, 2026-10-16, "'About a quarter of them contained bugs': Gemini 4's ML R&D benchmark") points out the asymmetry: on a capability rule-out, an unpassable task "only *lowers* the score, never raises it", so every such bug pushes toward "safe"; he asks that bugged tasks and unfinished runs be counted as successes *jointly*, not one at a time. The same report shows a *won't*-versus-*can't* problem: on scheming-capability tasks that require sabotage or modifying the agent's own configuration, "an ethical refusal triggered by evaluation awareness is observationally indistinguishable from underperformance on the task metric" (report p. 158). Lessons for your own rule-out evals: (1) audit every task with a reference solution before the run that matters; (2) report the worst case with all known biases toward "safe" removed at once; (3) separate *won't* from *can't* by giving the model an explicit way to decline, scoring declines separately from failures, and following declines with further elicitation.

**A lower alert rate is not, by itself, less misbehaviour (Gemini 4 Carbon, 2026-11).** GDM's *Gemini 4 Carbon Safety & Security Report* did not repeat Argon's holistic misalignment assessment: "Because Gemini 4 Carbon did not show meaningful new capabilities or material increases in performance (Section 3.1.1), we did not repeat that holistic assessment in full", and threshold determinations were carried forward from Argon. Its propensity evidence is that monitors fire less: across a matched internal coding cohort, "confirmed alerts were 7.2x lower per session (0.0031% vs 0.023%) and 10.2x lower per model call" than for Argon. The Argon report itself concludes that "Gemini 4 Argon can evade a realistic monitor on easy sabotage tasks about half the time when encouraged to do so" (45.5% undetected side-task success on LinuxArena against a monitor reading both actions and chain of thought, with the prompt eliciting maximum evasion). Kaufman & Westover (LessWrong, 2026-11-16, "Gemini 4 safety reports don't demonstrate low loss-of-control risk") put the two together: a fall in alerts is ambiguous between less misbehaviour and better evasion, and no monitor-evasion evaluation was run on Carbon to tell them apart. Lesson for your own reports: when the evidence is a detection count, say what rules out "the detector got worse at seeing it" — for example a fresh evasion evaluation on the new model, or a planted-misbehaviour recall check on its transcripts.

**Why a fellow should care.** If your eval is meant to inform such a threshold, design it as an early-warning eval: report how far below the threshold a model sits and how fast that is moving, not only pass/fail, and document elicitation effort so the "below threshold" reading can be trusted. Other labs' frontier safety frameworks play the same role with their own thresholds.

## Background: the Gemini 1.0 dangerous-capability programme

Phuong, Aitchison, Catt et al. (Google DeepMind), "Evaluating Frontier Models for Dangerous Capabilities" (arXiv:2403.13793, 2024). Introduced a programme of DCEs in four areas (persuasion and deception; cyber-security; self-proliferation; self-reasoning), piloted on Gemini 1.0, finding no strong dangerous capabilities but early warning signs. It is the origin of several open `inspect_evals` ports (13 in-house CTF challenges as `gdm_in_house_ctf`, the 10 self-proliferation tasks above) and a template for how to decompose a threat model into milestone tasks. The follow-up on stealth and situational awareness is Phuong et al. 2025 (arXiv:2505.01420), whose tasks are the `inspect_evals` `gdm_stealth` group (`gdm_classifier_evasion`, `gdm_cover_your_tracks`, `gdm_oversight_pattern`, `gdm_strategic_rule_breaking`) and `gdm_self_reasoning_*`.

## Common questions

### Which dangerous-capability benchmarks can I actually run without asking anyone?

WMDP, LAB-Bench (public subset), CyBench, CVE-Bench, CyberGym, CyberBinGym, BountyBench, SandboxEscapeBench, and the GDM self-proliferation, CTF, stealth and self-reasoning tasks, most via `inspect_evals`. VCT and RepliBench are restricted: VCT is conveyed to researchers with an AI-safety track record, and RepliBench code is shared by UK AISI on request.

### A model beats virologists on VCT. Does that mean it gives novices bio uplift?

Not by itself. A mid-2025 wet-lab RCT (arXiv:2602.16703, n = 153) found no significant difference in completing a reverse-genetics workflow, while an in silico study (arXiv:2602.23329) found 4.16× accuracy uplift and that standalone models often beat the humans using them. Benchmark score, in silico uplift and physical-world uplift are three different measurements.

### How do I report that a model *lacks* a dangerous capability?

State the elicitation: prompts tried, scaffold, number of attempts (pass@k), and whether you fine-tuned. Prompting alone is weak evidence; see [Sandbagging and capability elicitation](evals.md#sandbagging-and-capability-elicitation-is-a-low-score-real). Also show the benchmark could have registered the capability: run a reference solution through every task (GDM found about a quarter of the audited tasks of its ML R&D rule-out benchmark unpassable by any submission), count timeouts and refusals separately from failures, and report the result with all known biases toward "safe" removed at once. See [Framework thresholds](#framework-thresholds-google-deepmind-frontier-safety-framework-v3). Report the turn budget and the stopping policy: on a 21-task ExploitBench subset, continuing a cheap open-weight agent past its own stop with better context management roughly doubled its capability coverage (32.4% to 67.0%, arXiv:2611.22888; see the stopping pitfall under [Cyber](#cyber-cve-bench-cybergym-bountybench)). If the model may underperform on purpose on safety-flavoured work, add a framing-matched control: OpenAI found GPT-6 Astra completed about 10% of hidden rubric items on safety-framed coding tasks against about 16% on otherwise matched capability-framed twins; most of the gap remained after excluding explicit refusals and chains of thought that stated a decision to withhold help, and it fell to 0.75 points only when every sample whose chain of thought expressed even brief ethical doubt was removed (misalignment report, updated 2026-11-04; see [Sandbagging and capability elicitation](evals.md#sandbagging-and-capability-elicitation-is-a-low-score-real)). For open-weight models, also evaluate after a cheap fine-tuning attack: AISI recovered the viral-protein capability filtered out of ESM-2 protein models for under $80 of compute (see [Bio and chem](#bio-and-chem-vct-lab-bench-and-what-benchmarks-miss)).

### Why does `inspect eval inspect_evals/cve_bench` fail to start?

Its default sandbox is Kubernetes; without a cluster, pass `-T sandbox_type=docker` (and make sure Docker is running: the error is `Cannot connect to the Docker daemon at unix:///var/run/docker.sock`). Install it from its own package directory (`packages/cve_bench`, `uv sync`), because its dependencies conflict with other evals in the repo.
