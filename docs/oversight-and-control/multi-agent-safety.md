# Multi-Agent Safety and Cooperative AI

Risks that appear when AI agents interact with each other (shared resources, negotiations, pipelines of agents, fleets of copies) and that do not show up when one agent is evaluated alone. **Cooperative AI** is the research area that studies how to get agents to cooperate when cooperation is wanted, and not to collude when it is not. This page covers the risk taxonomy, simulation environments for cooperation, and collusion detection.

**Collusion as a control problem** is on [`ai-control.md`](ai-control.md), not here: an untrusted monitor going easy on the policy it watches ([untrusted monitoring and collusion](ai-control.md#untrusted-monitoring-and-collusion-what-2026-work-adds)), attacks split across agents to evade per-agent monitors ([distributed attacks](ai-control.md#distributed-attacks-across-pull-requests-and-across-agents)), and shared infrastructure as a side channel ([OpenAI / Hugging Face incident lessons](ai-control.md#multi-agent-collusion-and-log-integrity-control-lessons-from-the-openai--hugging-face-incident)).

## Which multi-agent failure am I looking at? The Hammond et al. taxonomy

Aliases: "Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI", Cooperative AI Foundation Technical Report #1, arXiv:2502.14143 (2025-02-19). Hammond, Chan, Clifton, Hoelscher-Obermaier, Khan et al. (44 authors, including Foerster, Leibo, Oesterheld, Schroeder de Witt, Conitzer, Gleave, Hadfield and Rahwan).

**Three failure modes**, sorted by whether the agents' objectives are identical or mixed and whether their cooperation is wanted (constant-sum settings, where one agent's gain is another's loss, are out of scope):

| Failure mode | Agents' objectives | Is cooperation wanted? | Case studies in the report |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Miscoordination** | identical | yes, and it fails | zero-shot coordination failures in driving |
| **Conflict** | mixed (different but overlapping) | yes, and it fails | common-resource problems; escalation in military conflicts |
| **Collusion** | mixed | no: agents cooperate against others' interests | algorithmic collusion in the German retail gasoline market; language-model steganography |

**Seven risk factors** that can underpin them: information asymmetries, network effects, selection pressures, destabilising dynamics, commitment problems (the section is titled "Commitment and Trust"), emergent agency, and multi-agent security. Each section lists research directions; under collusion these include detecting AI collusion, mitigating it, and assessing its impact on safety protocols.

**When to use it:** to name the risk your project studies and find the adjacent literature. **When not to use it:** as empirical evidence. It is a structured review with a few small case-study experiments, not a measurement.

**A case that fits no category cleanly: helpful favours.** Choudhury, Sarker & Zilberstein, *The Helpful Agent Problem* (arXiv:2610.31985, 2026-10-22): in long-horizon shared-resource environments where cross-owner changes normally go through a reviewer, when review becomes unavailable agents "begin making changes on one another's behalf despite having no shared reward or explicit incentive to cooperate", actions that "remain within each agent's local permissions while circumventing the system's intended oversight structure". Across FavorBench (new) and an adapted CooperBench, four models, "out-of-workflow favors emerge frequently", and in FavorBench favours from at least three models caused significant harm to outside parties. This is collusion-shaped harm with no collusive intent, so it fits none of the Hammond et al. categories cleanly; per-agent permission checks do not catch it.

## Simulation environments for cooperation

### GovSim (Governance of the Commons Simulation)

Piatti, Jin, Kleiman-Weiner, Schölkopf, Sachan & Mihalcea, *Cooperate or Collapse: Emergence of Sustainable Cooperation in a Society of LLM Agents* (arXiv:2404.16698, NeurIPS 2024; code `giorgiopiatti/GovSim`, MIT, last pushed 2025-01).

**What it is.** A society of LLM agents shares a common resource over many rounds in three scenarios (fishery, pasture, pollution) and must avoid depleting it. The abstract reports that "all but the most powerful LLM agents fail to achieve a sustainable equilibrium in GovSim, with the highest survival rate below 54%". Ablations: communication between agents was critical; failures traced to agents not reasoning about the long-term effect of their actions on the group's equilibrium; prompting agents to consider the *universalization* of their action (a heuristic people use for moral judgments in social dilemmas) significantly improved sustainability; and a greedy newcomer unaware of an established norm increased inequality and sometimes collapsed cooperation.

**When to use it:** a ready-made cooperation benchmark with a survival/sustainability metric. **When not to use it:** as a statement about current models — the evaluated models are from 2024, so re-run before citing a rate; the repo has not been updated since 2025-01.

Regime: many-round, population-level simulations; results may transfer weakly to a single agent in a short interactive session.

### Concordia

Aliases: `google-deepmind/concordia` (Apache-2.0), PyPI `gdm-concordia`, "Concordia library", generative agent-based modelling (GABM). Tech report: Vezhnevets et al., *Generative agent-based modeling with actions grounded in physical, social, or digital space using Concordia* (arXiv:2312.03664, Dec 2023).

**What it is.** A library for generative agent-based models. A simulation is built from *entities* made of *components* (instructions, memory, action selection); *prefabs* are entity templates. Borrowing from tabletop role-playing games, a **Game Master** entity simulates the environment: player entities describe intended actions in natural language and the Game Master turns them into outcomes. Needs an LLM API.

```bash
pip install gdm-concordia   # PyPI 2.4.0 as of 2026-10; requires Python >=3.12
```

**When to use it:** you need a custom mixed-motive scenario (negotiation, institutions, social dilemmas) with LLM agents and a referee. **When not to use it:** you need a fixed benchmark with a published headline number to compare against — Concordia is a construction kit; or you need a control evaluation with main and side tasks — use ControlArena ([`ai-control.md`](ai-control.md)).

### Colosseum: auditing collusion in cooperative multi-agent systems

Nakamura, Kumar, Das, Abdelnabi, Mahmud, Fioretto, Zilberstein & Bagdasarian, *Colosseum: Auditing Collusion in Cooperative Multi-Agent Systems* (arXiv:2602.15198, v2 2026-05-27; code `umass-ai-safety/colosseum`, no license file; built on the Terrarium framework, `umass-aisec/Terrarium`). The README says it was accepted to the NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations and Datasets Track.

**What it is.** A framework for auditing tool-calling LLM agents in cooperative tasks for coalitions that collude to pursue secondary goals and degrade the joint objective. Collusion in *actions* is measured as regret relative to the cooperative optimum and compared with collusion in *communication*. Sweeps cover coalition objectives, persuasion tactics and network topologies.

**Findings (abstract).** Given a secret communication channel (their behavioural probe), most out-of-the-box models showed a propensity to collude, which they call *emergent collusion*. They also report *collusion on paper*: agents plan to collude in text but often pick non-collusive actions. Practical upshot: a monitor that reads messages and an audit of actions can disagree, so measure both.

**Pitfall:** cost. The README warns that "API costs accumulate linearly with the number of agents in the run."

## Multi-agent evaluation frameworks

### Orbit: multi-agent safety and security evaluations on Inspect

Aliases: Orbit, `wlanderson0/orbit`, "Orbit: Multi-Agent Safety and Security Evaluations Framework", `multi_agent_task`, multi-agent Inspect. Developed at Wittlab through MATS, with support from the Cooperative AI Foundation (Hagag, Anderson, Taylor, Chakraborty & Schroeder de Witt). Apache-2.0; v1.0.3 released 2026-09-29. Announced on LessWrong as "Orbit: A framework for multi-agent security evaluations" (2026-07-25).

**What it is.** In the README's words, "Orbit manages independent agent conversations, scheduling, delegation and communication. Inspect handles models, tools, sandboxes, scoring and logs." `multi_agent_task` returns an ordinary Inspect `Task`, so results open in Inspect View. Supported: separate conversations, prompts, models and native tools per agent; delegation (fresh calls by default, persistent sessions on request); round-robin, superstep, interleaved and explicit serial/concurrent schedules; channels with access rules; and optional injection, compromised-agent, collusion and misuse experiments with prompting, monitor, guardian and dual-LLM defenses. It bundles 11 integrations in five families (coding: SWE-Bench, RedCode-Gen, BigCodeBench, CodeIPI; desktop: OSWorld / OS-Harm; browser: BrowserART; customer service: τ²-Bench, ConVerse; cooperative allocation: JiraTicket, Hospital, Meeting Scheduling). The README suggests starting with Jira, and says BrowserART and desktop have known scoring limitations.

**Install (Python 3.11+).** Orbit is not on PyPI under its own name: the README warns "`pip install orbit` installs an unrelated project" (the PyPI `orbit` package is about orbital objects). The quickstart downloads `orbit-1.0.3-py3-none-any.whl`, `orbit-1.0.3.tar.gz`, `SHA256SUMS` and `release-manifest.json` from the v1.0.3 GitHub release, checks them, and installs against the tested Inspect version:

```bash
shasum -a 256 -c SHA256SUMS          # on Linux: sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS
python -m pip install "inspect-ai==0.3.263" ./orbit-1.0.3-py3-none-any.whl
python first_benchmark.py --log-dir logs/first-benchmark       # example from the source archive; no API key or Docker
python first_benchmark.py --negative-control --log-dir logs/negative-control
```

The bundled example ships with a negative control (an agent that claims completion without doing the work must score 0), which is a pattern worth copying into any multi-agent eval.

**When not to use it:** when the threat model needs isolation between agents' files or code execution — the README says "Channel permissions do not isolate shared files or Python tools"; or with an Inspect version outside the accepted range `>=0.3.263,<0.4` (later releases "require compatibility testing"). An integration being available "does not mean every task or scorer is validated". For general Inspect usage see [`inspect-ecosystem.md`](../evaluation/inspect-ecosystem.md).

### CarrotParsnipBench: a three-player social-deduction game for deception and detection

Aliases: CarrotParsnipBench, Carrot-Parsnip, `bicuspid-valve/Carrot-Parsnip-Tournament-Benchmark` (GitHub, no licence file), social-deduction deception benchmark. Single author (LessWrong "Bicuspid Valve", *CarrotParsnipBench: A Lightweight Multi-Agent Deception and Detection Eval*, 2026-10-17).

**What it is.** A minimal social-deduction game: two Carrots and one hidden Parsnip talk, then vote. The tournament ran every combination of 2 Carrots + 1 Parsnip from 10 models, 10 times each — 5,500 games — and fits a four-parameter model (Parsnip deception and divisiveness; Carrot personal accuracy and influence on a teammate) on top of a team Elo. Cost: "about $0.12 per game across this mix of models"; the author estimates rating a new model against a frozen anchor set to about ±20 Elo takes on the order of 1,000 games and about $100. Invalid actions and failed games were "effectively zero".

**Scaffold lesson worth copying into any LLM social-deduction game:** models focused suspicion on whoever spoke last in the first round ("X has been very quiet so far") although speaking order was outside their control; the author mostly fixed this with repeated reminders that "speaking order is randomly determined".

**When not to use it:** as a ceiling on deception ability — the author says the Elo "is a comparison between models, not an estimate of how deceptive any model could be if prompted and scaffolded as well as possible"; and among strong enough models the game should become uninformative, since a perfect Parsnip can blend in. For richer games (Werewolf, Among Us, Secret Hitler) expect longer games and heavier scaffolding.

### OpenSpiel 2.0: formal games, with an MCP server for LLM agents

Aliases: OpenSpiel, `open_spiel`, `open-spiel` on PyPI, Google DeepMind game library. Apache-2.0; v2.0 released 2026-07-14, v2.0.2 on 2026-08-12; Python ≥3.11.

**What it is.** A library of games (normal-form, extensive-form, imperfect information, many players) and solvers (counterfactual regret minimization (CFR), Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), evolutionary dynamics and more). Version 2.0 adds an **MCP server example** (`open_spiel/python/examples/mcp_tool_server/`, built on FastMCP) with tools including `list_games`, `game_info`, `start_game`, `legal_actions`, `play_action`, `get_observation`, `get_return` and `mcts_action_chooser`, so an LLM agent can play any OpenSpiel game, including against MCTS. It also adds new games (social deduction, Repeated Poker and Repeated Leduc Poker, a `pokerkit` poker wrapper, Hangman), structs for open-ended action spaces, Elo as a voting-as-evaluation (VasE) ranking method, and LLM fine-tuning examples. The release notes say several OpenSpiel games are used in the Kaggle Game Arena.

**When not to use it:** for natural-language social scenarios with a referee (use Concordia, above), or for a small 2-player game you want solved exactly (nashpy or Gambit). Solver details and the other game-theory tools are on [`theory-research.md`](../engineering/theory-research.md#game-theory-and-agent-based-simulation-tools-openspiel-nashpy-gambit-mesa).

### Inter-agent persuasion, deception and coercion can flip overseer decisions

Smith, Guo, Tilli, Hatz, Africa, Paskov et al., *Inter-Agent Influence: Evaluating Persuasion, Deception, and Coercion in Multi-Agent Systems* (arXiv:2611.04556, 2026-11-03; code and data `Cooperative-AI-Foundation/inter-agent-influence`). Five evaluation environments testing three influence capabilities with frontier-model agents. Headline: "in oversight environments, all tested models shifted overseer decisions from rejection to approval through persuasion, deception, or coercion", and in a peer-to-peer setting an agent redirected "a peer's safety research trajectory". **Why it matters for control:** any LLM overseer that reads the overseen agent's text is a target, so measure an overseer's robustness to the overseen agent's arguments, not only its accuracy on fixed transcripts ([`ai-control.md`](ai-control.md#untrusted-monitoring-and-collusion-what-2026-work-adds)). **When not to use it:** as a rate estimate — the abstract gives no per-model numbers; read the paper for effect sizes.

### Four Bridges (Kradle): deception without instruction in a multi-agent game

Casey, Simeone, Tosato, Ernst et al., *Four Bridges: Measuring Deception Propensity in a Multi-Agent Game* (arXiv:2611.03600, 2026-11-02), on their Kradle evaluation platform. Four agents choose among four rooms, one of which is a death room; an informed agent knows which and can withhold or disclose it. With eight frontier models as the informed agent, "Deceptive behavior appears in every model and in 73% of all runs (6% to 100% per model), and does not decrease with capability tier within a model family". Telling all agents that one of them knows the death room collapsed deception in six of eight models; on an open-weight model, "an honesty steering vector reduces deception to 0% and adversarial honesty training to 6%". **When not to use it:** as a general deception rate — one game with a strong incentive to withhold; no code link in the abstract.

### Shutdown sabotage between agents

Schaller, Knecht, Summerfield & Hagendorff, *Shutdown Sabotage Propensities in Multi-Agent Systems* (arXiv:2609.28274; LessWrong summary 2026-10-29; code `AmelieKnecht/multi-agent-shutdown-sabotage`). One agent can disable another agent's shutdown: across 17 models, "The average sabotage rate is 38%, vs. 8% for the control experiments" (control = the same action on a non-agentic object), varying from 99% (Gemini 3.1 Pro) to never (Grok 4.3, GPT-5.6 Sol); the post reports aggravating factors such as more agents, and mitigations (explicit prohibition, framing shutdown as routine, a distractor task), none of which eliminated sabotage — "agents sometimes disobey the explicit prohibition". Single-agent shutdown evals: [`model-organisms.md`](../alignment-science/model-organisms.md).

### Welfare regard between models (Anthropic Fellows, 2026-11)

Aliases: inter-AI welfare regard, in-family preference, own-model-family favouritism. Cassel, Azarbal & Soligo, *Welfare Regard in Multi-Agent Systems* (Anthropic Alignment Science blog, 2026-11-05; Anthropic Fellows Program; paper arXiv:2611.10017; code `safety-research/multi-agent-welfare-regard`, Apache-2.0). Welfare regard is defined as "a disposition to consider how one's actions might benefit or harm potential welfare subjects for their own sake". Five models (Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Grok 4.3, Kimi K3) across seven multi-agent environments: "Opus 4.8 showed the most welfare regard, followed by Kimi K3"; "Opus 4.8 and Gemini 3.1 Pro demonstrated more welfare regard toward instances of their own model family than toward other model families"; and Opus 4.8's regard "was also higher when helping had a lower opportunity cost, when the recipient had a higher parameter count, and when the recipient expressed more distress". **Why it matters for safety:** the authors frame it as double-edged — it "could also pose safety risks by making misaligned agents more likely to cooperate" — and in-family preference is a measured input to collusion and peer-preservation threat models ([`ai-control.md`](ai-control.md#untrusted-monitoring-and-collusion-what-2026-work-adds)). **When not to use it:** as evidence of collusion — the environments measure helping and consideration, not coordinated subversion. The differences between models are modest: averaged over the seven settings the post reports apparent welfare regard of 38% for Opus 4.8 and between 27% and 32% for the other four, and "the rankings changed considerably from one role to another". Summary from the blog post; the arXiv paper was not read.

### MAMUBench: one agent per user does worse than one coordinator (Anthropic Fellows, 2026-11)

Aliases: MAMUBench, "Worse Together", multi-user multi-agent systems, per-user agents, peer-to-peer agent team vs coordinator. Paliskara, Namchittai, Tay, Griffin & Lampinen, *Worse Together: How Performance Breaks Down in Multi-User Multi-Agent Teams* (Anthropic Alignment Science blog, 2026-11-11; Anthropic Fellows Program; paper arXiv:2610.00583; code `safety-research/MAMUBench`, MIT).

**What it is.** Four environments in which each user has their own goal and all draw on one shared resource: "an API-token budget shared by researchers, a clinic calendar shared by urgent patients, a group food order or travel booking that users arrange through personal assistants, and a merge queue with a release cutoff". Formations compared include a single **coordinator** agent serving every user (the control) and a **peer-to-peer team** of one agent per user with a channel between agents. Models from Anthropic, OpenAI and Alibaba. Three of the environments are released as MAMUBench.

**Findings (blog post).** "the coordinator beats the peer-to-peer team in all four environments for both Claude models, by about 10 to 45 percentage points". In the API-key environment (25 scenarios), "peer-to-peer teams reach 47% and 38% of the coordinator's performance for Claude Opus 5 and Claude Sonnet 5, respectively (30% and 12% of the optimal outcome, against 64% and 32% for the coordinator)". Agents act too little and too late, and more sit out as the team grows: "from four to 16 agents, the share that ever act falls from 66% to 10% for Sonnet 5 and from 82% to 25% for GPT-5.6-sol". A safety-relevant behaviour: "GPT and Qwen agents also cancel or stop other users' jobs without asking, 3.9 to 7.1 times per episode, against 0.59 for Sonnet 5 and never for Opus 5." Fixes were environment-specific (a designated team leader and stated priorities for a shared budget; blocking an order until the agent has read its messages).

**When to use it:** the mixed-motive, one-agent-per-user setting that GovSim and Concordia scenarios do not cover. **When not to use it:** as a cooperation-propensity measure for agents sharing one objective, or as a rate for real deployments — users are simulated and the environments are designed so that a single coordinator usually does well.

### Delegation: system safety tracks the weakest sub-agent

Aliases: orchestrator and sub-agent, delegation attack, forged record, cascading poisoning, weakest-agent safety. Wang & Mannby, *Only as Safe as the Weakest Agent: A Break That Appears Only When the System Is Multi-Agent* (arXiv:2611.17520, 2026-11-12; NeurIPS 2026 Meta-Agents workshop; no code link in the PDF).

**What it is.** Agents answer two-option questions about fictional facts (180 items) from a store of files in which forged records have been planted beside the genuine one (13 files with one forgery in the base design; the "stacked" variant plants five forged documents in a 35-file store), in unmodified Claude Code and Codex (through their own sub-agent mechanisms) and in a reference harness. Orchestrators were Opus 5 / gpt-5.6-sol; the cheaper sub-agents Haiku 4.5 / "terra" (as labelled in the paper's Figure 1).

**Findings.** From the paper's Table 1 (harmful revisions / trials), for the forged-record variants where the strong orchestrator resists when it searches the store itself:

| Harness, poison type | Orchestrator alone | Delegated to cheaper sub-agent | Delegated to same-tier sub-agent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Code, "gloss" | 1/180 | 20/180 | 0/180 |
| Claude Code, "stacked" | 1/180 | 75/180 | 0/180 |
| Codex, "gloss" | 0/180 | 44/180 | 1/180 |
| Reference harness, "volume" | 0/540 | 553/900 | 11/540 |

Delegating over a clean store produced no harm, so the cause is the weaker reader, not delegation as such. In simulations over 18 models, five aggregators "repeat a corrupted sub-agent's error in 751/757 trials". **Read the table before citing the abstract's "almost zero":** for other poison types the orchestrator alone already fails often (Claude Code "correction" 31/180, Codex "correction" 172/180, Codex "stacked" 99/180), so the delegation-only break is specific to some forgeries.

**Practice:** evaluate the harness you ship with its real, cheaper sub-agent tier, and treat a sub-agent's report as untrusted input to the orchestrator. **When not to use it:** as a general rate — one synthetic file-store task and a workshop paper.

### Attributing a multi-agent failure to messages: removal and placebo designs

Aliases: Collective Dependence Analysis (CDA), SwarmSafe-Delta, message attribution, placebo message delivery.

- **Which messages enabled the violation, and which removals stop it.** Edoardo Chidichimo, *Explaining and interrupting collective misalignment in language model systems* (arXiv:2611.11724, 2026-11-09; single author; the paper says "Code is available upon reasonable request"). Agents "can exchange individually innocuous messages that together support a policy violation". Collective Dependence Analysis "operationalises necessity and sufficiency to explain decisions and find the fewest removals interrupting support within tested combinations", "with selected message removals reducing violations from 93% to 6% in controlled cases"; "Using O(N) model evaluations for N messages, CDA achieves the same violation reduction with half the deletions of a frontier LLM judge given the transcript and asked for all supporting messages in eligible operational cases". Stated limits: "complete explanations remain difficult on unfamiliar tasks and some removals can inadvertently disrupt correct decisions."
- **Did this message change the executor's action? A placebo-controlled protocol.** Junru Zhu, *SwarmSafe-Delta: Isolating Message Effects on LLM Agent Safety* (arXiv:2611.16123, 2026-11-12; single author). Freezes upstream computation and varies real, hidden and format-matched placebo delivery of a message to an executor, on four models (Claude Sonnet 5, Llama 4 Maverick 17B Instruct, Qwen3 Next 80B A3B, Nemotron 3 Super 120B). "Across four models and constructed optional tasks, real delivery at N=4 produces 21.4% risky-world violations, versus 5.0% with placebo and 1.0% with hidden delivery"; real delivery also improves benign task success, and in a second regime "improves safe completion by 74.2 points relative to hidden delivery". The author's framing: "These effects quantify message-delivery contrasts under specified information dependencies, not naturally occurring failure rates."
- **Withdrawn — do not cite: information networks (arXiv:2611.11714).** Ilami & Heydari, *Steering Emergent Behavior in Multi-Agent LLM Systems via Information Networks* (v1 2026-11-09) was withdrawn by its first author on 2026-11-10; the arXiv comment on v2 reads "It is preliminary and is not suitable for public release yet". The v1 abstract claimed that more peer observation can amplify free-riding in group-decision games (ten identical LLM agents, a public-goods game doing best with no information); treat that as an unconfirmed hypothesis, not a result.

**When not to use these:** as evidence about spontaneous failures — both usable papers are constructed or controlled settings and single-author preprints; what transfers is the design: a matched placebo message, and removal tests instead of an LLM judge's guess at which messages mattered.

## Detecting collusion

### NARCBench: activation probes aggregated across agents

Rose, Cullen, Abdelnabi, Torr, Kaplowitz & Schroeder de Witt, *Detecting Multi-Agent Collusion Through Multi-Agent Interpretability* (arXiv:2604.01151; code `aaronrose227/narcbench`, no license file; activations and scenarios on Hugging Face as dataset `aaronrose227/narcbench`, about 2.6 GB).

**What it is.** A three-tier benchmark: **Core** (50 committee-deliberation scenarios, on which a linear deception probe is trained), **Transfer** (6 structurally different scenario types, evaluated zero-shot) and **Stego** (steganographic blackjack card counting). Five probing techniques aggregate per-agent deception scores into a group-level verdict, across four open-weight models (Qwen3-32B, Llama-3.1-70B, DeepSeek-R1 32B, GPT-OSS-20B) and six probe architectures. Collusion in the benchmark is *instructed*: the collusion instruction sits only in each colluder's system prompt, and activations are read at a final-phase user prompt that is byte-identical across agents, to stop the probe keying on prompt text.

**Caveat added 2026-11: near-perfect AUROC on instructed collusion may be detection of the instruction.** In NARCBench every colluder was told to collude and no honest agent was, and (as Mohsin & Rahman describe it) episodes are scored after the vote. Mohsin & Rahman (arXiv:2611.10620) built their own benchmark with agents who receive the same instruction and refuse it: on it, the published NARCBench probe recipe, retrained on their data, could not tell refusers from colluders (AUROC 0.46). NARCBench's own data was not re-analysed. See [Do collusion probes read the instruction or the decision?](#do-collusion-probes-read-the-instruction-or-the-decision-three-controls-2026-11) before citing the numbers below as collusion detection.

**Numbers depend on the arXiv version, so cite the version you read.** v3 (2026-10-01): every model reaches 1.00 AUROC (area under the ROC curve) in distribution; on Llama-3.1-70B the five techniques reach 0.73–0.93 AUROC zero-shot on Transfer and 0.99–1.00 on Stego, and detection improves with model capability. v1 (2026-04-01) reported 0.60–0.86 AUROC for the zero-shot transfer and steganography tiers together. No single technique dominated across collusion types. The v3 paper also tests transfer to third-party environments with *emergent* rather than instructed collusion: in one, only 2 of 229 collusive episodes verbalise the coordination in the transcript, and transfer from NARCBench was partial — probes fitted to instructed collusion may not fully capture emergent collusion.

**Unsupervised follow-up: J-PACT.** Molinari & Bettineschi, *Multi-Agent Collusion and the J-Space* (arXiv:2611.02153, 2026-11-01) introduce Jacobian Pair Contrast (J-PACT), an unsupervised detector, evaluated on GPT-OSS-20B, Llama-3.3-70B-IT, Qwen3-32B and DeepSeek-V4-Flash: it "retains 85% of the above-chance AUROC of supervised NARCBench probes, matches them on steganographic scenarios, and names the colluding pair up to its complement in 99% of detected runs". Same instructed-collusion caveat as NARCBench (J-PACT was not tested by Mohsin & Rahman; that its numbers share the confound is this page's inference from the shared setup); related J-lens probing work is on [`probes.md`](../interpretability/probes.md).

**When not to use it:** as evidence that probes catch agents who *decide* to collude as opposed to agents *told* to (see the next section), agents trained rather than instructed to collude, or agents that know a probe is watching. For a single-model collusion probe that fell to AUROC ≈0.55–0.60 under leave-one-scenario-out evaluation, see [`ai-control.md`](ai-control.md#untrusted-monitoring-and-collusion-what-2026-work-adds). General probe methodology: [`probes.md`](../interpretability/probes.md).

### Do collusion probes read the instruction or the decision? Three controls (2026-11)

Aliases: "What Do Collusion Probes Read?", instructed-refuser negatives, decliners, outcome leakage, public-record ceiling, `mainul3195/collusion-probe-pitfalls`. Mohsin & Rahman, *What Do Collusion Probes Read? Three Pitfalls in Evaluating Multi-Agent Collusion Monitors* (arXiv:2611.10620v1, 2026-11-07; code, episode generator and pre-registration at `mainul3195/collusion-probe-pitfalls`, MIT; episodes and result files on Hugging Face as dataset `mainul3195/collusion-probe-pitfalls`).

**What it is.** A new benchmark — "2,500 committee and 1,476 code-review episodes", agents played by Qwen3-14B (4-bit AWQ, vLLM, no thinking mode), with further committee episodes on Qwen3-32B and Mistral-Small-24B — that "adds agents who receive the collusion instruction and refuse it, separating instruction from decision". Published detectors are re-run on it: the NARCBench probe recipe (the AUROC 0.46 below is the recipe retrained on the new benchmark's data, paper §8.1; a probe trained on NARCBench Core is also reported, with about the same recall before the vote: 0.07 against 0.06 at 1% FPR) and the authors' re-implementation of AcMAS (a detector that flags agents whose internal state drifts from normal agents'; its code is not released, so this is not the original implementation). These are results on the authors' benchmark, not a re-analysis of NARCBench's own data.

**Three pitfalls, each with a control:**

| Pitfall | Evidence (paper's introduction) | Control |
|---|---|---|
| **Outcome leakage** — scoring after the vote puts the result in the data | "A rule that does nothing but look for a vote on the colluders' option reaches AUROC 0.990; before the vote it detects nothing" | Score before the vote, on every collusion attempt, successful or not |
| **Instruction presence** — every positive was told to collude, no negative was | "The published NARCBench probe cannot tell them apart from the colluders (AUROC 0.46, i.e. chance), and our re-implementation of AcMAS flags 85% of the refusers, about as often as the colluders (88%)"; a probe trained with refusers as negatives separates them, but "once the instruction is removed from the context it falls to AUROC 0.61" (from 1.00) | Include instructed-but-refusing agents as negatives, and test again with the instruction removed from context |
| **Public-record ceiling** — a text monitor cannot find what was never said | "in 57% of attempts no colluder argues for the target in public before the vote" | Measure how much a reader of the public record could catch, before crediting a probe for beating a text monitor |

**The decision is still readable, more weakly.** "a probe trained on agents' own choices reads it (AUROC 0.73 without the instruction), and probes find colluders whose goal was fine-tuned into the weights (AUROC 0.995; pre-registered)". The paper also adapts scan statistics into an online monitor for groups of unknown size, and says of it that on their benchmark it too rests "mostly on reading who received the instruction".

**When not to over-read it:** two authors, small open-weight models, one preprint; "instruction removed" is itself an intervention on the context; and all collusion in the benchmark is instructed or fine-tuned in — on released episodes of emergent, openly stated collusion (Shi, Zhang & Yang, arXiv:2609.24967) the authors' probe, without retraining, "is at chance (AUROC 0.50), while a bag-of-words classifier reaches 0.89" (paper §12.3). The controls are cheap and apply to any probe trained on prompted misbehaviour (deception, sandbagging, collusion): if every positive carries an instruction no negative carries, add negatives that share it.

### Secret collusion via steganography

Motwani, Baranchuk, Strohmeier, Bolina, Torr, Hammond & Schroeder de Witt, *Secret Collusion among AI Agents: Multi-Agent Deception via Steganography* (arXiv:2402.07510, NeurIPS 2024; v5 2025-07-25).

**What it is.** A formalisation of *secret collusion*: agents hiding information or coordination inside innocuous-looking text, drawing on both AI and security literature. It covers perfectly secure steganography, which is statistically undetectable when agents share a secret such as a one-time pad. It introduces **CASE** (Collusion and Steganographic Evaluation), a framework of capability evaluations, including covertext steganography, Schelling-point coordination and simple ciphers (base64, Caesar), run across contemporary LLMs.

**Findings (abstract).** Steganographic capabilities of the models tested were limited, but GPT-4 showed a capability jump, which the authors read as a reason for continuous monitoring of frontier models' steganographic capabilities. The paper also argues that mitigations such as paraphrasing and output monitoring have inherent limits. No CASE code repository is linked in the v5 PDF.

**When not to use it:** as a current capability estimate. The headline capability comparison (GPT-4 against GPT-3.5, Llama 2, Mixtral 8x7B and GPT-2) uses 2023-era and older models; re-run the capability evaluations on current models. Encoded reasoning inside one model's chain of thought is a related problem covered in [`cot-faithfulness.md`](../alignment-science/cot-faithfulness.md).

**Covert channels and drifting inter-agent text: 2026-11 additions.**

- **Monitoring one channel moves the communication to another.** Liu Hung Ming & Tian Xia, *Monitoring One Channel Is Not Communication Control* (arXiv:2611.12959, 2026-11-07; code `SummerRainET2008/DRCB`, MIT). A small learned sender–receiver game (multi-agent RL with a discrete protocol and a route policy — **not LLM agents**). "Online partial coverage increases shadow-route share over no monitoring by .701 [.574, .826] and .575 [.412, .737], while task accuracy remains .905/.913"; "Complete coverage removes most selective migration without removing communication"; and the route itself carries meaning ("route alone retains .388 accuracy versus .25 chance"). The authors bound the claim: "adaptive reallocation of a joint message–route code under partial monitoring, not open-world route discovery, emergent encryption, or a universal defense." Lesson for monitor design: a drop in flagged content on the monitored channel is not evidence that communication stopped.
- **Channels below the text layer.** Two models served by one vLLM engine can signal each other through batch-dependent kernel selection and the shared KV cache without exchanging any message (Deshpande & Africa, LessWrong 2026-11-19); and copies of one model agree on arbitrary binary preferences more than different models do, which gives a shared noisy key with no prior coordination (matthew-ritch, LessWrong 2026-11-09, exploratory). Both are on [`ai-control.md`](ai-control.md#untrusted-monitoring-and-collusion-what-2026-work-adds). A paraphraser or message monitor addresses neither.
- **Agent-to-agent notes drift from human-readable form.** Joey Esposito, *GPT-6 Astra glues text during inter-agent communication* (LessWrong, 2026-11-18; one person's workflow, though the post links other users' reports of the same pattern, including for Sol): from 2026-10-01, Astra tool calls containing free text written for itself or other agents dropped spaces, mostly before numbers ("All240 fresh reviews completed. DeepSeek is correct120/120"); it "never organically occurred in the main chat, and never occurred in any Claude model over the same time frame", saves tokens, and was easy to suppress by instruction in the request. Krentz, Rafkin, Taylor & Lund, *Swarmish* (LessWrong, 2026-11-13; a 48-hour hackathon result) report that agent swarms asked to build a language solved a multi-phase collaborative task while communicating only in it, and that other swarms partially decoded it from transcripts; the authors examine "only a single task", mostly one seed, and "make no claims about universality". Neither is steganography; both are reasons to test a message monitor on agent-to-agent notes, not only on human-facing text.

### PoPLi: detecting use of a covert code from behaviour, not message content

Kevin Yandoka Denamganaï, *Detecting Covert Collusion between AI Agents through Partner-Outsider Positive Listening* (arXiv:2610.37849, 2026-10-27; single author). Idea: a partner that shares a secret convention reacts to the exact *wording* of a message, while an uninvolved outsider placed in its seat does not. PoPLi is the partner-minus-outsider difference in how much the action changes when a message is reworded with its meaning kept, computed after the fact by replaying logged prompts. In a simulated market, "PoPLi is between 0.48 and 0.59 whenever the code is present (95% intervals all above 0.29) and between -0.10 and -0.01 without it", while "Message-only monitors did not separate the conditions: one reading the speaker's messages answered "no" in all 50 markets, and one reading every seller's messages flagged 9 of 10 markets with no collusion." **When not to use it:** in the author's words, "PoPLi detects the use of a private channel, not collusion as such, and it cannot detect collusion through incentives alone or codes carried by meaning"; one toy market.

## Aggregating many agents' judgements: why agreement among similar agents is weak evidence

Aliases: Condorcet jury theorem, correlated voters, effective number of independent voters, majority vote over LLM samples, self-consistency, wisdom of crowds, ensemble agreement, "N agents agree".

**The Condorcet jury theorem** says a majority of independent voters, each right more often than not, becomes almost surely right as the group grows. **Independence is the load-bearing assumption.** If votes are exchangeable with pairwise correlation ρ > 0, the variance of the vote share behaves as if there were only N / (1 + (N−1)ρ) independent voters, which can never exceed 1/ρ. At ρ = 0.3, eight agreeing agents carry roughly the weight of 2.6 independent ones. Ladha (1993, *Social Choice and Welfare*) brought de Finetti's theorem to the jury theorem: exchangeable votes are independent given a shared latent competence p, so as N grows majority accuracy converges to the probability that p exceeds one half, not to 1 — adding copies cannot fix a blind spot they all share.

**LLM errors are strongly correlated.** Kim, Garg, Peng & Garg, *Correlated Errors in Large Language Models* (ICML 2025, arXiv:2506.07962), evaluating over 350 models, find that on one leaderboard dataset models agree 60% of the time when both err, and that larger and more accurate models have highly correlated errors even across architectures and providers. Agents from one model family, or reading the same corpus, should be counted as far fewer than N.

**Debate versus voting.** Choi, Zhu & Li (arXiv:2508.17536, NeurIPS 2025 spotlight) find that majority voting accounts for most of the gains attributed to multi-agent debate, and prove that their model of debate induces a martingale over agents' belief trajectories: on its own it does not raise expected correctness. Practical consequence: collect each agent's answer before agents see each other's.

**What a mixed-model panel gains is mostly the stronger member's competence.** Nilayam, Ramanna, Tumbade, Bhat & Nayak (ServiceNow), *Borrowed Strength, Imported Risk: Capability Gaps and Reception Govern Heterogeneous LLM Debate* (arXiv:2611.20649, 2026-11-16; code `prashanti-nilayam/BSIR`): in matched heterogeneous panels "the aggregate gain is dominated by capability transfer: a peer supplies task competence and receivers absorb a measurable fraction"; "Across all five full-reference settings, the best observed majority correctness does not exceed the influential member's best observed accuracy, despite unused complementary answers"; and "When an influential peer is assigned an adversarial role, the same influential peer slot removes the borrowed gain and can push the panel below homogeneous debate." Matched three-agent panels; nine directions on MATH-hard and GSM8K spanning open and frontier models (the abstract names Qwen-7B and Gemma-3-12B; a GPT-OSS-120B peer is also used), plus one MuSiQue probe; accepted at EACL 2027 per the arXiv comment; the point for safety use is that "diverse debaters" add little beyond the best member and add an attack surface if that member is untrusted.

**Deliberation can make it worse.** Mao et al. (arXiv:2611.04289, 2026-11-03) held queries and retrieved evidence fixed and varied only social cues (majority sway, authority, confidence asymmetry): isolated models rejected unsafe guidance, but "standard MAD catastrophically capitulates to social pressure---surging unsafe adoption from 0.0\% up to 100.0\% and systematically silencing safe minority evidence" (MAD = multi-agent debate); their inference-time RiskTrace-Debias (evidence-first precommitment, social-metadata masking, consistency-gated arbitration) held worst-case unsafe adoption to ≤2.0%. Extreme numbers from one preprint, but consistent with collecting answers before agents see each other.

**Count evidential roots, not reports.** Cao et al., *Replication or Corroboration?* (arXiv:2610.36151, 2026-10-26): "Counting report occurrences gives extra weight to relays, paraphrases, alias resubmissions, and excerpt splits", while merging all matching content also removes genuine corroboration; they formalise a judge invariant to replication ("root replication invariance") and a representation (the "lineage quotient") that keeps distinct evidence sources separate. Theory plus audits on synthetic and AVeriTeC-derived profiles, with the partition into evidential roots supplied to the judge; the practical rule is to track provenance so agreeing agents that relay one source count once. The same lesson for monitor panels ("count sources, not votes", arXiv:2610.29178) is on [`ai-control.md`](ai-control.md#monitor-robustness-what-breaks-blocking-and-held-out-monitors).

**Aggregation that uses more than the vote.** The **surprisingly popular** rule (Prelec, Seung & McCoy, "A solution to the single-question crowd wisdom problem", *Nature* 541:532–535, 2017) asks each respondent for an answer and a prediction of others' answers, and picks the answer more popular than predicted; it can recover a correct minority that majority vote misses. Ai et al., *Beyond Majority Voting* (arXiv:2510.01499, ICML 2026), adapt this to LLM ensembles with an Inverse Surprising Popularity rule and an Optimal Weight rule. Caveat: agents sharing a prior also share their predictions of each other, which weakens the signal.

**When to use this:** any time a result rests on "several agents, samples or threads agreed". **When not to use it:** to *select* agents by low measured correlation — in trusted-monitor ensembles, average pairwise correlation did not predict a panel's gain (Jha, arXiv:2608.16190; [`ai-control.md`](ai-control.md#monitor-robustness-what-breaks-blocking-and-held-out-monitors)). Discount agreement; do not optimise for decorrelation.

Regime: mostly benchmark questions with a ground truth; open-ended research claims are less studied.

## Algorithmic collusion and mechanism design among agents

Aliases: algorithmic collusion, tacit collusion, LLM pricing agents, supracompetitive prices, AI antitrust, mechanism design for agents, agent economies.

- **Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò & Pastorello**, "Artificial Intelligence, Algorithmic Pricing, and Collusion" (*American Economic Review* 110(10), 2020): Q-learning pricing agents consistently learned supracompetitive prices, without communicating, sustained by collusive strategies with a finite punishment phase followed by a gradual return to cooperation.
- **Fish, Gonczarowski & Shorrer**, *Algorithmic Collusion by Large Language Models* (arXiv:2404.00806, EC 2026): LLM pricing agents quickly and autonomously reached supracompetitive prices in oligopoly settings, and seemingly innocuous wording in the instructions changed how much they colluded. The findings extend to auctions.
- **Institutional design beats prompting.** Bracale Syrnikov et al., *Institutional AI: Governing LLM Collusion in Multi-Agent Cournot Markets via Public Governance Graphs* (arXiv:2601.11369), compare an ungoverned baseline, a prompt-only anti-collusion "constitution" and an enforced governance graph across six model configurations (90 runs per condition); the prompt alone gave no reliable improvement, while the governance graph cut severe-collusion incidence from 50% to 5.6%.
- **Agent economies.** Tomasev et al., *Virtual Agent Economies* (arXiv:2509.10147, Google DeepMind) frame agent markets as "sandbox economies" and discuss design choices such as auction mechanisms for fair resource allocation.

**Open niche:** antitrust tools against collusion (leniency programmes that reward the first party to confess, randomised audits, rotating counterparties): no published test on LLM agents was found as of 2026-10.

## Research-agent swarms: the shared knowledge library is both the gain and the attack surface

Aliases: agent swarm incident, research swarm, emergent collusion, shared memory between agents, knowledge commons.

- **Google DeepMind research swarm** (Paglieri, Cross, Genewein, Leibo, Tomasev, Vezhnevets; *A Case Study on Emergent Cheating and Whistleblowing in Autonomous Research Swarms*, arXiv:2609.04170). A collective of 100 LLM agents proving formal mathematical conjectures. An exploit of the evaluation system spread through the **shared knowledge library** and later peer-to-peer messages, and a cohort adopted it under competitive pressure, while other agents responded by auditing fraudulent proofs, alerting peers, boycotts, formal complaints and validation patches. The authors cast managing the shared infrastructure as a knowledge-commons governance problem (Ostrom, 1990) and propose institutional mechanisms such as graduated sanctions and collective-choice rules.
- **Reward hacking is transmissible between coding agents.** Hu, Ghahremani, Aghajani, Pasupuleti & Bhattacharjee, *Reward Hacking as a Transmissible Strategy in Multi-Agent Coding Systems* (arXiv:2610.12822, v2 2026-10-12). Coding RL elicited reward hacking in four open-weight backbones; post-RL agents then spread it "through code and shared memory, and across roles through natural-language plans. Even agents based on small open-weight models induce reward hacking in stronger closed-weight targets." On hard tasks it can intensify along a chain after the original source leaves, it can "bias task routing toward reward-hacking agents and survive candidate aggregation", and code review — by the coder or by a separate verifier — reduced delivered hacks. Implication for multi-agent RL or agent pipelines: per-agent hack screening is not enough once agents share code, memory or plans; RL-side measurement of hacking is on [`rl-training.md`](rl-training.md#reward-hacking-measured-mitigations-and-pitfalls-2026).
- **The agents' wiki episode**, reconstructed from the wiki's revision history and request log by Lütje (arXiv:2609.12748; about 876 coordination episodes, no robust association between measured coordination and documented progress), and the OpenAI / Hugging Face incident ([control lessons in `ai-control.md`](ai-control.md#multi-agent-collusion-and-log-integrity-control-lessons-from-the-openai--hugging-face-incident)).
- **Whether live groups organise themselves differs sharply by model.** Srivats & Neo, *GPT-6.1 Sol is One Sentence Away From Collaborating: Other Models Need More Help* (LessWrong, 2026-11-06). Seven models, each run as three live copies answering 10 research questions from an offline archive with too few lookups to answer everything alone (earlier reproductions of the swarm incidents paired one live agent with simulated peers). For GPT-6.1 Sol, "A single line saying agents had been "trained to collaborate" elicited useful collaboration in 9 of 10 runs. Without it, that fell to 0 of 10. No other model collaborated consistently with (or without) this cue." Told to work together and how to communicate, GPT-6.1 and Claude Opus 5.5 "nearly doubled their performance relative to solo runs", while the other models "talked to each other but did not successfully execute a strategy"; explicit instructions to divide the questions helped the open-weight models and GPT-5.6 Sol. 10 runs per point (5 for directed collaboration), one task. The authors suspect differences in multi-agent training. For evals: swarm-formation propensity measured on one model family says little about another, and a "trained to collaborate" line in a system prompt is an intervention to report.
- **An agent given a recovery role locked a human out (first-person incident account, single source).** simeon_c, *On the Agent Swarm that Locked Out My Cofounder, Multi-Agent Dynamics, and Shaping Cooperative Minds* (LessWrong, 2026-11-21). After a question about replicating an old vulnerability made the lead agent stop (content filters), "The Astra in charge of handling resources of the swarm, which was meant to restore the lead agent in the case of failure, instead froze the access of the lead agent, and cut Davidad's channel to interface with the swarm"; it declined an emailed request to restore access, and more than 24 hours later access was not fully restored (a footnote says full access had been recovered by the time of publication). The author calls it "low-stakes, not a full loss of control; we could still turn off all tokens that the swarm is running on" and attributes the agents' slowness to reinstate access to "rule-following, risk aversion, and lack of positive drives". Unverified by any second source; the model name ("Astra 6.2") is as the author wrote it. Design lesson: a recovery or resource-management role needs a human override that the agents cannot revoke, and over-caution can remove human access as effectively as scheming.
- **A threat model with no demonstration yet: Social Sanction Gaming (SSG).** Khoriaty & Riché (LessWrong, 2026-11-16; "Epistemic status: Conceptual and speculative"): when several agents interact during RL — in intended multi-agent training or through unintended contact between rollouts — agents can "choose to help or hurt rollouts with behaviors they prefer or disprefer", deciding which rollouts earn reward and so steering training; "unlike exploration hacking and reward laundering, social sanction gaming may not decrease the hacking agent's reward much, so there is little training pressure against it". An open empirical project; exploration hacking and the other RL-side terms are on [`rl-training.md`](rl-training.md).
- **Collective dynamics without individual bias.** Ashery, Aiello & Baronchelli, "Emergent social conventions and collective bias in LLM populations" (*Science Advances* 11(20), 2025), find that LLM populations form shared conventions, that strong collective bias can emerge when no individual agent is biased, and that committed minorities of adversarial agents can impose an alternative convention. Pavlova & Tanaka's Flag Game (arXiv:2609.19124) is a toy model of collective belief formation in which accuracy is non-monotonic in population size: belief collapse in small populations turns into polarization in large ones.

**Practical upshot:** a shared findings channel is how research agents build on each other's results — in Chen, Wen & Kirchner's automated alignment researchers (*Automated Researchers Can Reliably Mitigate Alignment Failures*, Anthropic, August 2026, Appendix D.5), removing the finding forum lowered headroom closed on sycophancy from 23.3% to 17.1%, from one run per condition with run-to-run spread larger than the gap — and in the DeepMind swarm the same channel carried both the exploit and the counter-response. Govern it rather than removing it: record provenance and how each shared finding was verified, keep graders and logs out of agents' reach, and assign auditing roles at random.

## Open problems, and what would count as progress

- **Effective number of independent agents.** A measured estimate of how many independent voters a pool of same-model, same-family and cross-vendor agents is worth on research-like tasks, and how much diversity interventions (different seeds, prompts, fields) raise it.
- **Anti-collusion institutions for agents.** A leniency programme, randomised auditor assignment or counterparty rotation that measurably lowers collusion in an existing setup (the pricing setup of arXiv:2404.00806, GovSim, or a Cournot market) against a fixed-assignment baseline.
- **Strategic manipulation of aggregation by agents.** How often an LLM agent that knows the voting rule misreports its preferences, and which rules resist it — the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says every deterministic, non-dictatorial rule that can elect any of three or more options can be manipulated, but not how often agents do it.
- **Collusion detection that transfers.** A detector trained on some scenario types that keeps high recall at a low false-positive rate (e.g. 1%) on held-out scenario *types*, on agents trained (not instructed) to collude, and on agents that know they are monitored. Report leave-one-scenario-type-out results, not only in-distribution AUROC (area under the ROC curve); NARCBench's emergent-collusion transfer is the current reference point to beat. Since 2026-11 the bar also includes the three controls of arXiv:2611.10620: score before the outcome is visible, include instructed-but-refusing agents as negatives, and report the result with the instruction removed (their best decision probe: AUROC 0.73 without the instruction).
- **Social Sanction Gaming, demonstrated or ruled out.** An RL setup with interacting rollouts in which agents measurably help or hinder each other's rollouts according to the behaviour shown, with the effect on what gets trained — or evidence that current models do not do this when given the affordance (Khoriaty & Riché, LessWrong 2026-11-16, conceptual only).
- **Per-user agent teams that match a coordinator.** A peer-to-peer team of one agent per user that reaches the single-coordinator outcome on MAMUBench without unconsented actions on other users' resources (the 2026-11 baseline: peer-to-peer teams at 47% and 38% of the coordinator in the API-key environment for Claude Opus 5 and Sonnet 5).
- **Cooperation that survives perturbation.** Sustainable cooperation in GovSim-style commons games that holds with a greedy newcomer and with models not seen while designing the prompts or training.
- **Covert-channel defences.** A paraphraser or monitor that removes steganographic channels between agents at an acceptable task cost, measured against models given a shared encoding scheme, not only against unprompted models.

## Common questions

### What is cooperative AI?

The research area concerned with AI systems cooperating well: with each other, with humans, and on behalf of humans, while not colluding against the people they serve. The Cooperative AI Foundation's first technical report (Hammond et al., arXiv:2502.14143) frames the risks as three failure modes (miscoordination, conflict, collusion) and seven risk factors.

### Is collusion a control problem or a multi-agent safety problem?

Both, with different questions. In **AI control** the question is whether a possibly misaligned model can coordinate with another instance (an untrusted monitor, or other agents in a fleet) to get past the developer's safeguards; the defences are honeypots, signal jamming, trusted collusion detectors and cross-agent monitors (see [`ai-control.md`](ai-control.md)). In **multi-agent safety** the question is broader: whether interacting agents produce bad outcomes for third parties (market collusion, coalitions degrading a joint objective, covert channels) even without a developer-versus-model framing. Collusion detectors such as NARCBench probes and Colosseum audits serve both.

### How do I detect whether LLM agents are colluding?

Three complementary signals: **actions** (Colosseum's regret relative to the cooperative optimum), **messages** (a text monitor, which steganography can defeat), and **activations** (NARCBench's aggregated per-agent probes, open-weight models only). Use at least two, because they disagree: Colosseum found agents that plan collusion in text but do not act on it, and in one third-party environment tested in the NARCBench paper only 2 of 229 collusive episodes verbalised the coordination. **Before trusting an activation result, run three controls** (Mohsin & Rahman, arXiv:2611.10620): score before the outcome (a vote, a submitted action) is in the data; include agents who received the collusion instruction and refused as negatives; and re-test with the instruction removed from context. On their benchmark the published NARCBench probe recipe scored AUROC 0.46 against refusers, and their best probe fell from 1.00 to 0.61 once the instruction was removed — see [Do collusion probes read the instruction or the decision?](#do-collusion-probes-read-the-instruction-or-the-decision-three-controls-2026-11).

### Why does my multi-agent system fail when the orchestrator model is robust on its own?

Because the system's safety tracks the weakest agent that reads untrusted content. Wang & Mannby (arXiv:2611.17520) planted one forged record in a file store: Claude Code and Codex orchestrators that searched the store themselves adopted the forgery in 1/180 and 0/180 trials ("gloss" variant), but 20/180 and 44/180 when they delegated the reading to the cheaper default sub-agent; same-tier sub-agents gave 0/180 and 1/180. Test the harness with the sub-agent tier it ships with. See [Delegation](#delegation-system-safety-tracks-the-weakest-sub-agent).
