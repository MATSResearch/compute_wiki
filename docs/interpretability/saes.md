# Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)

Tooling for training and using SAEs (Sparse Autoencoders) on language model activations. SAEs decompose activations into a large dictionary of sparse, ostensibly more-monosemantic features. Related architectures: **transcoders** (decompose MLP I/O), **crosscoders** (decompose across layers or models), **JumpReLU SAEs**, **TopK SAEs**, **Gated SAEs**.

## Why SAEs? Superposition and feature geometry

Aliases: "superposition", "polysemanticity", "Toy Models of Superposition", "TMS", "linear representation hypothesis", "feature geometry", "strong superposition".

The case for Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) rests on a theory of how models store features. **Elhage et al., *Toy Models of Superposition*** (Anthropic, arXiv:2209.10652, 2022; also transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model) gives a toy model in which polysemantic neurons arise because the network stores more sparse features than it has dimensions, as nearly-orthogonal directions ("superposition"); it reports a phase change in whether features are stored this way, a connection to the geometry of uniform polytopes, and evidence of a link to adversarial examples. If real features are sparse linear directions that outnumber dimensions, an overcomplete dictionary trained with a sparsity penalty — an SAE — is the natural tool for recovering them. Every SAE result is therefore partly a test of that assumption; the skeptic's list below is the evidence that recovery is incomplete.

- **Superposition and scaling laws.** Liu, Liu & Gore, *Superposition Yields Robust Neural Scaling* (arXiv:2505.10465; NeurIPS 2025 Best Paper Runner-up) vary superposition in the toy model with weight decay: with weak superposition, loss follows a power law in model size only if feature frequencies are power-law distributed; under strong superposition "the loss generically scales inversely with model dimension". They report that open-source LLMs are in the strong-superposition regime and that Chinchilla scaling is consistent with this.
- **Not every structure is a bag of independent directions.** Computational-mechanics work finds activations organized as *belief-state geometry* (sometimes fractal) and as factored orthogonal subspaces — see [`slt-devinterp.md`](slt-devinterp.md). Attention has its own superposition problem, which Lorsa (below) targets.
- **Ground truth for testing.** SynthSAEBench (below) builds superposition, hierarchy and correlation into a synthetic model with known features, so you can check whether an architecture recovers them before spending on LLM runs.
- **Concept manifolds, and a tool to test for them.** Bhalla, Fel, Rager, Feucht, Haklay, Wurgaft, Boppana, Kowal et al., "Do Sparse Autoencoders Capture Concept Manifolds?" (arXiv:2604.28119, 2026-04-30; code `goodfire-ai/sae-manifold`, MIT) give a framework in which an SAE can capture a low-dimensional manifold *globally* (a compact group of atoms whose span contains it) or *locally* (features that each tile one region), and find that SAEs mix the two in a fragmented regime they call *dilution* — one reason manifold structure is rarely visible in single features. The repo ships two evaluations: **subspace capture** (does the SAE's feature set span the subspace of a known manifold — colors, temperature, age, geography, days, years, formality, sentence length — or shatter it?) and **unsupervised clustering** of features by decoder cosine, co-activation, correlation, mutual information or Ising couplings. It defaults to Llama-3.1-8B and a minimal `BatchTopKSAE`; another SAE needs only `encode`, `decode` and decoder access (README). Quickstart: `uv run data.py --manifold colors`, then `uv run subspace_capture.py plot --sae /path/to/sae.pt --k 64`. A sibling repo, `goodfire-ai/block-sparse-featurizer` (MIT, no releases), lifts the unit of sparsity from directions to **blocks** (small subspaces) with Vanilla, Grassmannian and Group-Lasso variants, e.g. `bsf.GrassmannianBSF(d=768, n_groups=256, group_size=3, l0=16)` then `bsf.train(model, x, epochs=60)`; its demo is on DINOv3 *vision* features. **When not to use them:** production SAE training (both are research code); language-model BSF claims (the shipped demo is vision-only).

**Open problem.** Whether LLM features really are the sparse linear directions the toy model assumes — or a mixture of linear directions, multi-dimensional manifolds and hierarchies that a flat dictionary splits arbitrarily (see "feature splitting" below) — is unsettled. A convincing result predicts, before looking, a measurable property of a pretrained LLM's activations or of its SAEs (e.g. how dictionary quality scales with width) from a superposition model, and confirms it.

## Should I use SAEs at all? (the skeptic's reading list)

Before committing a MATS project to Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), read the 2025–2026 negative-results literature. SAEs are popular but contested, and several careful studies find they often fail to beat simple baselines. This matters for project scoping: if your goal is to *act on a concept you can already name* (e.g. "find and ablate the deception direction"), a plain linear probe may match or beat an SAE at a fraction of the cost (see [`probes.md`](probes.md)).

- **Kantamneni et al. 2025**, "Are Sparse Autoencoders Useful? A Case Study in Sparse Probing" (arXiv:2502.16681; ICML 2025; Subhash Kantamneni, Josh Engels, Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Max Tegmark, Neel Nanda). Tests SAEs on activation probing across data scarcity, class imbalance, label noise, and covariate shift. Finding: SAE latents do **not** reliably beat standard probing baselines on raw activations. If probing is your goal, don't assume SAEs help.
- **Leask et al. 2025**, "Sparse Autoencoders Do Not Find Canonical Units of Analysis" (arXiv:2502.04878; ICLR 2025; Patrick Leask, Bart Bussmann, Joseph Bloom, Curt Tigges, Lee Sharkey, Neel Nanda). Uses **SAE stitching** to show SAE dictionaries are *incomplete* and **meta-SAEs** to show latents are *not atomic* (a latent decomposes into finer latents). This is the theoretical backbone of the "feature splitting" pitfall below — there is no single "true" feature set to recover.
- **Korznikov et al. 2026**, "Sanity Checks for Sparse Autoencoders: Do SAEs Beat Random Baselines?" (arXiv:2602.14111). Sharpest negative result: a **random baseline** matched fully-trained SAEs across interpretability (0.87 vs 0.90), sparse probing (0.69 vs 0.72), and causal editing (0.73 vs 0.72). Always include a random/untrained-dictionary baseline in your SAE evals. **A counter-analysis of its synthetic case study** (not of the random-baseline results above): Celene Nightingale, "Sanity Checks for Sanity Checks for Sparse Autoencoders" (LessWrong, 2026-10-31; independent replication, unreviewed) argues the paper's synthetic-recovery failure (d = 100, 3,200 features, L0 20) is a BatchTopK dead-latent artifact. She reports that the paper's own Appendix B shows "TopK recovers **99.9% of features**", replicated it ("TopK recovered 99.6% of features"), and with per-sample normalization as in the original BatchTopK, "BatchTopK achieved 99.3% recovery … This also fixes JumpReLU with 97.2% feature recovery"; she notes the ICML listing's updated abstract mentions the TopK result, and flags that her modified environment does not match real models either. Her suggested health check: "how much better they are at reconstructing real data than they are at reconstructing random vectors with in-distribution norms". When an SAE fails in a toy, check the dead-latent rate before generalizing.
- **Peng et al. 2025**, "Use Sparse Autoencoders to Discover Unknown Concepts, Not to Act on Known Concepts" (arXiv:2506.23845; Kenny Peng, Rajiv Movva, Jon Kleinberg, Emma Pierson, Nikhil Garg). The reconciling framing, and a useful project-scoping heuristic: SAEs are a good tool for **discovering unknown concepts** (open-ended exploration, auditing, surfacing structure you didn't know to look for) but a poor tool for **acting on known concepts** (where a targeted probe or steering vector is better). Pick the tool to match which of those you're doing.

- **Wu, Arora, Geiger, Wang, Huang, Jurafsky, Manning & Potts 2025**, "AxBench: Steering LLMs? Even Simple Baselines Outperform Sparse Autoencoders" (arXiv:2501.17148; ICML 2025 spotlight; `stanfordnlp/axbench`). On Gemma-2-2B/9B, "for steering, we find that prompting outperforms all existing methods, followed by finetuning. For concept detection, representation-based methods such as difference-in-means, perform the best. On both evaluations, SAEs are not competitive." Details and the current leaderboard are in [`steering.md`](steering.md#axbench-benchmark-steering-against-prompting).
- **Chanin 2026**, "Are Sparse Autoencoder Benchmarks Reliable?" (arXiv:2605.18229). The benchmarks themselves are noisy: two SAEBench metrics fail an audit (see [SAEBench](#saebench) below).
- **A cheap non-SAE baseline: ICA Lens** (below). Independent component analysis recovers interpretable, token-selective directions without training a dictionary; worth running first and reporting next to any SAE result.
- **Many transcoder features are MLP neurons in disguise.** Shabalin & Piotrowski (Anthropic Fellows / Anthropic), "Comparing Sparsified MLPs and Transcoders: Many Transcoder Features Are Neurons in Disguise" (arXiv:2610.24521, 2026-10-17; NeurIPS 2026 workshop *Interpretability as a Science*). A *neuron feature* is a transcoder feature whose encoder matches a gated-MLP neuron's `W_up ± W_gate` and whose decoder matches its `W_down` at cosine > 0.7. Across 13 open-source transcoders (per-layer ones on Gemma 3 1B–27B, Qwen 3 1.7B–14B and Llama 3.1 8B, plus the two `mntss` cross-layer transcoders on Llama 3.2 1B and Gemma 2 2B, per the paper's Table 4), neuron features were 14–59% of *active* features in the second half of the layers (over 50% for most Gemma 3 and Qwen 4B–14B transcoders; paper Table 9). Abstract: "Only neuron features have a stable correspondence with something in the underlying MLP: other features vary more between training runs, do not match the MLP's Jacobian, and change less significantly from their random initialization." TopK-thresholded, sign-split MLP neurons lose to transcoders on reconstruction (FVU, fraction of variance unexplained); untrained, they are also worse on downstream KL divergence, but with a trained linear map on the pruned MLP output they beat transcoders on KL (FVU stays higher); they match or exceed transcoders on sparse probing; transcoders win on fine-grained concepts such as bigrams. The authors' recommendations: avoid transcoders with expansion factor below 4× ("for MoE models, this practically means not using transcoders at all"); compare any dictionary against a neuron baseline "thresholding the neurons with TopK of around 64, and considering positive and negative activations of a given neuron as distinct semantic units"; study more closely the features that reproduce across seeds and align with the original MLP's computation. Scope (their limitations): base models, mostly English data, dense models only, mostly per-layer transcoders (the cross-layer analysis covers only the two `mntss` models). Their TopK transcoders are released under MIT.
- **MLP neurons as a circuit basis.** Arora, Wu, Steinhardt & Schwettmann (Transluce), "Language Model Circuits Are Sparse in the Neuron Basis" (arXiv:2601.22594; ICML 2026 spotlight): "MLP neurons are as sparse a feature basis as SAEs"; their gradient-attribution pipeline on raw neurons found that "a circuit of ≈10^2 MLP neurons is enough to control model behaviour" on a subject-verb agreement benchmark. Code: `TransluceAI/circuits` ("ADAG: Transluce's MLP neuron-level circuit tracing library"; no license file, last push 2026-04-10). MIB's causal-variable track reached a similar verdict ("SAE features are not better than neurons"; see [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#mib-the-mechanistic-interpretability-benchmark)).
- **Update 2026-11: the neuron-basis case now has a technical report and an independent line of evidence.** (1) Goodfire's companion report to the essay in the next bullet is out: Tigges, Kowal, McGrath, Lewis & Cammarata, "Neurons as an Interpretable Basis" (2026-11-10, https://www.goodfire.com/research/neurons-as-an-interpretable-basis), on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (a mixture-of-experts model). Its sparsity claim, as worded on the page on 2026-11-23: "Ablating the 92.2% weakest MoE neuron activations for each forward pass results in a sparse model very close to the original, with only slight (1.2-2.2 points) degradation in log-likelihood benchmarks without generated reasoning", with a light fine-tune restoring reasoning benchmarks (the "1.2-2.2 points" are benchmark points, not a loss percentage; a LessWrong linkpost quotes an older abstract with "a 2.9% increase in language model loss", which is not on the page now). It also reports that most neurons have one causally dominant "mode", that agent-written descriptions predict firing "with 95% accuracy vs random text" and recover 73% of ablation damage by simulation in high-activation regimes, and that "8 neurons recover as much function as 128-256 random directions". Relevant to transcoders: its appendix classifies the features of transcoders trained on the same model — besides neuron copies, most are one neuron narrowed by a read-side condition or "coalitions" of co-firing neurons, where "the transcoder feature represents a semantic combination or intersection of the neurons it is related to, rather than novel unrelated features"; features with no same-layer match are described as "mostly echoes of other layers' neurons or fixed phrases". The authors add that this "does not rule out features spread diffusely across many neurons". (2) Shone & Dunlap (Anthropic Fellows / Anthropic), "Returning to the Neuron Basis for Interpretability" Parts 1–2 (2026-11-23), read neuron *output vectors* through a J-lens on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B/14B and OLMo-3-32B: neurons adding two unrelated concepts at once were about 1% or fewer of sampled middle-layer signed directions; and in Part 2, "for concept detection, neurons chosen from their descriptions and readouts beat sparse-autoencoder latents chosen from their descriptions, alone or with the same readouts". Their stated limits: readability under one lens "is only a proxy for interpretability", models of 8–32B only, and label-free neuron probes fell far behind 40-label probes on one set (AUROC 0.49 vs 0.95). Details, numbers and the remaining gap to Leo Gao's evidence bar are in [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#agenda-framing-pragmatic-ambitious-and-open-problems). Practical consequence: a **neuron baseline is now cheap enough to be expected** next to any SAE or transcoder result — both poles, thresholded, with a lens readout where a lens exists.
- **Contested (2026-10): are neurons "already clean"?** Nick Cammarata (Goodfire), *Neural Networks Are Already Clean* (essay, 2026-10-15, https://already-clean.goodfire.com/, plain text at `/essay.md`; LessWrong linkpost x4kvabkF2Jk8GLZp8) argues that canonical polysemanticity was mostly tool and architecture failure plus "recursive generalization", not superposition, and reports from "preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B" that "roughly 90% of neuron poles have one main job … another ~7% have two related jobs; and only ~2-4% are not yet understood". He calls the picture "our leading hypothesis", says the title "overstates the evidence, because we haven't audited everything", notes the share "depends on where you set thresholds", and deferred details to a companion paper (released 2026-11-10; see the update bullet above — it studies Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, not Qwen3-32B). Leo Gao, *Neural networks are not already clean* (LessWrong, 2026-10-17), accepts that polysemanticity was overestimated but states the evidence bar: an explanation counts only if you can **simulate** the neuron from it and substitute the simulated values without degrading the model — "fixed basically entirely by (a) measuring downstream KL instead and (b) scoring on a large dataset" — and reports his "best guess" that "ablating small neuron activations quite substantially degrades model performance" (results promised, not yet shown). Treat the 90% figure as an unreplicated, threshold-dependent working hypothesis; the claim would be settled by downstream-KL simulation scores on a large dataset for a random sample of neurons. A low-confidence data point on the explainer side: Jasper Blank, "Is GPT 2XL already clean?" (LessWrong, 2026-10-25) re-ran OpenAI's 2023 explain-then-simulate protocol on GPT-2 XL neurons with Claude Opus 5.5 as both explainer and simulator (a 0–10 rating instead of GPT-4's probability-weighted simulation) and reports that "Opus 5.5 is able to identify 3-19% of the neurons", against about 0.3% scoring ≥ 0.8 for GPT-4 — on 50 neurons from layers 8–40, with the pipeline tuned on the first 20; a changed simulator and same-model judging make it not a like-for-like comparison. Practical checks from the essay worth using now, whoever is right: inspect a gated-MLP neuron's **negative pole** as well as its positive one (in one Qwen attribution graph in the essay, removing the eight neurons with a "Santa" negative side dropped the Santa answer "from near-certain to 6%", versus 76% after removing the twenty labelled Christmas/Santa); in SwiGLU, split activations by **sign regime** before labelling, because gate < 0 × value < 0 also yields positive activations (for "about 4% of neurons, it's the whole top of the list"; in one Qwen3.6-35B-A3B "enemy" neuron "131/133 responses in p75–p99 use − × −" while the strongest tail was + × +); and test a label with **blind-maximum** and **near-miss** examples written from the explanation alone (of 200 random neurons from the essay's Qwen3-32B gallery, 183 had positive-side explanations; new examples fired them strongly 76% of the time, near misses 11%).

- **Predictive SAE features need not be the error mechanism.** Nourbakhsh et al., "From Error Prediction to Causal Influence: Auditing SAE Features in Multiple-Choice QA" (arXiv:2610.38067, 2026-10-27; Gemma 3 1B/4B with Gemma Scope SAEs, eight datasets): after filtering features correlated with format and answer-order artifacts, the remaining features have more causal leverage than random ones, "However, answer flips are rare, and we do not observe stable effect directions across meaning-preserving changes in prompt format and answer order." Filter artifact-correlated features and test effect stability across prompt formats before calling a feature "the error mechanism".

**Bottom line:** SAEs remain valuable for open-ended discovery and circuit analysis, but for "I know the concept, I want to detect/steer it" tasks, baseline-check against probes (detection) and prompting (steering) first. For transcoder or circuit work, add a sign-split, TopK-thresholded **MLP-neuron baseline** scored on downstream KL, not only on reconstruction.

## ICA Lens (independent component analysis as a first lens)

Aliases: ICA Lens, ICALens, `icalens` on PyPI, `liusida/ica-lens-paper` on GitHub, FastICA for LLMs, independent component analysis, "interpreting language models without training another dictionary". Paper: Sida Liu & Feijiang Han, arXiv:2606.11722 (2026-06-10; the current listing, v3 of 2026-11-17, adds Sarah Wiegreffe and Furong Huang as authors), first titled "ICA Lens: Interpreting Language Models Without Training Another Dictionary"; the arXiv listing's title is now "ICA Lens: Non-Gaussianity Reveals Interpretable Directions in Language Models" (arXiv API, 2026-11-23).

**What it is.** A GPU-parallel PyTorch FastICA pipeline with stability recipes for LLM activations (no SciPy/scikit-learn dependency), fitting diagnostics, an interactive token-level explorer (Jupyter/Colab, or `result.to_html(...)`), component profiles (top examples, logit-lens tokens, optional R-lens readouts), and steering or clamping of ICA components. The intuition from the abstract: many interpretable directions are selective on tokens, so they look less Gaussian than random directions, which is what ICA finds. Published lenses on Hugging Face (collection `sida/ica-lens`) include GPT-2 small (`sida/icalens-gpt2-small-pile10k`) and Qwen3.5-2B chat (`sida/icalens-qwen3.5-2b-ultrachat-1m`); the paper reports GPT-2 Small, Gemma 2 2B and Qwen 3.5 2B Base. Release v0.4.0 and PyPI `icalens` 0.4.0 (both 2026-09-29); PyPI is 0.4.1 as of 2026-11-23 (uploaded 2026-11-13; release notes not read; its metadata pins `transformers==5.14.1`, so give it its own environment); MIT.

**Pre-fitted ICA lenses in SAELens format (Neuronpedia, 2026-11-07).** `neuronpedia/icalens-{gpt2-small, gemma-2-2b, gemma-2-2b-it, qwen3.5-2b-pt, qwen3.5-9b-pt, qwen3.6-27b}` on Hugging Face (`library_name: saelens`). The Qwen3.6-27B card: "Neuronpedia fit this lens with `icalens` 0.4.0 and converted it to SAELens format" — 1,000,000 tokens of `HuggingFaceH4/ultrachat_200k` rendered with the chat template, "FastICA (parallel, `logcosh`), 50 iterations, 5120 components per layer", layers 0–63, each folder `blocks.L.hook_resid_post/` "a SAELens `StandardSAE` with `d_sae = d_in = 5120`". That makes it an all-layer, dictionary-shaped artifact for Qwen3.6-27B that loads with SAE tooling — but it is an ICA basis with as many components as dimensions, not an overcomplete sparse dictionary, and its reconstruction and interpretability were not evaluated here.

```python
# From the README (pip install icalens); the two README blocks joined
from icalens import ICALens

lens = ICALens.from_pretrained("sida/icalens-gpt2-small-pile10k")
result = lens.analyze("She deposited the check at the bank.", layer=6)
result.to_html("analysis.html")
```

Fitting your own: `icalens fit text --model openai-community/gpt2 --dataset NeelNanda/pile-10k --layers 6 --token-budget 1000 --max-iter 20 --output icalens-output/gpt2-demo` (or `icalens fit chat ...` for instruction-tuned models), then `icalens profile`. Steering: `lens.generate(messages, layer=5, steer=(188, -12.0), max_new_tokens=16)`; the README warns that "component labels, signs, and suitable targets must be established empirically for the exact Lens and layer".

**Reported results (abstract):** "On SAEBench, ICA is competitive with public SAEs in sparse probing and outperforms them in targeted probe perturbation under small-to-medium budgets." Read the TPP half with the SAEBench audit in mind — Targeted Probe Perturbation is one of the two metrics that audit says should not be used to rank SAEs.

**When to use it:** a first look at a layer before training or downloading an SAE; a baseline row in any SAE paper; quick steering experiments on a small model.

**When *not* to use it:** you need more directions than the activation dimension (standard ICA yields at most `d_model` components — whether `icalens` relaxes this was not checked); you need compatibility with circuit-tracer, Neuronpedia or SAELens tooling; as headline evidence on TPP.

## SAELens

Aliases: `sae-lens` on PyPI, `decoderesearch/SAELens` on GitHub (formerly under `jbloomAus`), "Joseph Bloom's SAE library", "the SAE Lens library". Maintained by Joseph Bloom, Curt Tigges, Anthony Duong, David Chanin. Latest **v6.56.1 (2026-11-20**, also on PyPI). **Since v6.55.0 (2026-11-05) SAELens supports TransformerLens 4.x**: PyPI metadata for 6.55.0 onward declares `transformer-lens>=3.0.0,<5.0.0` (it was `>=2.16.1,<4.0.0` through 6.54.14), so it now installs alongside TransformerLens 4 and no longer alongside TransformerLens 2.x. (The description of PR #739 says it "keeps the `transformer-lens<4.0.0` pin"; the published metadata says otherwise and is what `pip` uses.) **Upgrade to ≥6.55.1 if you use Llama Scope SAEs, to ≥6.55.0 if you splice Gemma Scope 2 affine (skip) transcoders into a model, to ≥6.54.14 before comparing `ce_loss_score` / `kl_div_score` with anyone else's, to ≥6.54.2 if you load Gemma Scope 2 affine transcoders at all, and to ≥6.54.9 if you train BatchTopK SAEs with activation-norm scaling** (silent bugs, below).

**What it is.** The default community library for working with pretrained SAEs and for training new ones. Provides:

- `SAE.from_pretrained(...)` to load hundreds of community-trained SAEs (Gemma Scope — Google DeepMind, Lieberum et al., arXiv:2408.05147; Llama Scope — OpenMOSS, arXiv:2410.20526; GPT-2 small, Pythia, etc.)
- `HookedSAETransformer` — the same class that used to live in TransformerLens (removed there in TransformerLens 2.0 and moved into SAELens); it splices SAEs into a model's residual stream
- A training loop with the standard architectures: vanilla L1, TopK, JumpReLU, Gated; recent additions (release notes) include an **AbsTopK** bidirectional SAE (6.53.0, 2026-10-02), a covariance-whitening normalization option (6.52.0), the quadratic sparsity loss from Gemma Scope 2 for JumpReLU SAEs (6.51.0), `fold_W_dec_norm=True` to fold decoder norms at load time (6.54.0), and final L0 / dead-feature counts recorded in SAE metadata (6.47.0)
- `SAETransformerBridge` (needs TransformerLens 3.0+; the only splicing class on TransformerLens 4.x) — the `TransformerBridge` counterpart of `HookedSAETransformer`, for models the old hand-ported TransformerLens list did not cover (its docstring cites Gemma 3). Per PR #739 (v6.55.0): on TransformerLens 4.x `sae_lens.HookedSAETransformer` raises an `AttributeError` that points to `SAETransformerBridge`, and `model_class_name="HookedTransformer"` raises an error; the new `model_class_name="TransformerBridge"` loads the model with `TransformerBridge.boot_transformers` — "unprocessed HF weights, with TransformerLens hook names". Several 6.55.x releases fix bridge-path bugs (hooks after an SAE is removed, `device_map` loading, legacy TransformerLens-format models, the evals CLI on TransformerLens 4), so use the latest patch release on this path
- The training-buffer option `n_batches_in_buffer` was renamed `n_sequences_in_buffer` in v6.56.0 (PR #756; the old name remains as a deprecated alias, behaviour unchanged). The value has always counted *sequences* (`n * context_size` activations), so the old name "overstated the buffer by a factor of `store_batch_size_prompts`" — relevant when sizing a replay buffer (see "How many unique activations does SAE training need?" below)

**When to use it:**

- You want to use existing pretrained SAEs (this is the dominant use case for MATS fellows).
- You want to train an SAE on a model that's already supported by TransformerLens.
- You're integrating SAEs into a TransformerLens or HuggingFace pipeline.

**When *not* to use it:**

- You want the absolute fastest TopK training on big activations — **EleutherAI sparsify** is built for that.
- You want non-standard architectures (transcoders, crosscoders) — sparsify and dictionary_learning are more flexible.

**Pitfalls:**

- **`ImportError: cannot import name 'HookedTransformer' from 'transformer_lens'` on `import sae_lens`** (SAELens issue #738, 2026-09-22) — TransformerLens 4.0.0 (2026-09-21) removed `HookedTransformer`, and SAELens releases before 6.51.2 declared `transformer-lens>=2.16.1` with no upper bound. SAELens 6.51.2 (2026-09-24) pinned `transformer-lens<4.0.0`, and **6.55.0 (2026-11-05) added real TransformerLens 4 support** ("feat: support transformer-lens 4.x (requires transformer-lens 3.0+)", #739; metadata `transformer-lens>=3.0.0,<5.0.0`). Fix: upgrade `sae-lens` to ≥6.55.0 (latest 6.56.1), or `pip install "transformer-lens<4"` if you must keep `HookedSAETransformer` code unchanged. The earlier advice on this page to stay on TransformerLens 3.x for SAE work no longer applies; code written against `HookedSAETransformer` needs porting to `SAETransformerBridge` to run on TransformerLens 4.
- **Llama Scope SAEs fired about 35% fewer latents than in training — SAELens < 6.55.1 (no error; first affected version not stated).** Per PR #782: "The JumpReLU threshold that `llama_scope_sae_huggingface_loader` builds for Llama Scope SAEs ignores the decoder norm, so loaded SAEs fire about 35 % fewer latents than in training." The released SAEs were trained with the gate on `hidden_pre * ||W_dec[i]||` and their decoder rows are not unit-norm, so latents with large decoder norm were suppressed too often and those with small norm fired too often. Measured in the PR on `llama_scope_lxr_8x` / `l15r_8x` (Llama-3.1-8B base, `blocks.15.hook_resid_post`, 24,480 Wikitext-103 test tokens): L0 31.55 → 48.36 (trained k = 50), explained variance 0.675 → 0.715, never-fired latents 39.9% → 29.7%, Jaccard overlap of active sets with the training-time TopK-50 forward 0.59 → 0.82. Fixed in v6.55.1 (2026-11-10). **Any feature-level analysis of Llama Scope SAEs loaded through SAELens before 6.55.1 used the wrong active sets — re-run it.** Check: mean L0 on in-distribution text should be near the trained k.
- **Skip transcoders were spliced into the model without their skip connection — SAELens < 6.55.0 (no error or warning).** Per PR #768, the wrapper that `HookedSAETransformer` and `SAETransformerBridge` use to splice an SAE into the model output `decode(encode(x))`, but a `SkipTranscoder` adds its skip term in `forward()`, "so skip transcoders, including every Gemma Scope 2 `*_affine` transcoder (loaded as `JumpReLUSkipTranscoder`), are spliced in without their skip connection and without a warning". How much that matters: "at 270m-pt layer 5 the transcoder explains 0.988 of the MLP-out variance with it and 0.189 without it. With `use_error_term=True` the logits are unchanged, but `hook_sae_error` absorbs the skip term." This is a different bug from the transposed-skip loader bug fixed in 6.54.2 (next bullet): that one affected the standalone transcoder's output, this one affects *spliced* runs. Calling the transcoder directly (`transcoder(x)`) was correct. Fixed in v6.55.0 (2026-11-05); the PR does not say when the bug was introduced beyond naming an earlier change (#637) as the cause. Re-run spliced-replacement experiments and any analysis of `hook_sae_error` for skip transcoders made on older versions.
- **`run_evals` in bfloat16 / float16 rounded CE and KL metrics to about three significant digits — SAELens < 6.55.1.** Per PR #781, with the language model in bfloat16 or float16 the reported `ce_loss_*` and `kl_div_*` metrics (and so `ce_loss_score` and `kl_div_score`) "are rounded to that dtype. In bfloat16 that is about three significant digits, which is coarser than the differences between two SAEs." In its tiny-stories-1M example the CE gap with and without the SAE was reported as 0.0625 when the float64 mean was 0.0418, and `ce_loss_score` read 1.010526 instead of 1.007038. Fixed in v6.55.1; float32 runs were unaffected. The same release stops `run_evals` returning all-NaN `feature_density` lists when density statistics are off (PR #779) and fixes loading sparsify checkpoints (`size mismatch for W_dec`, or `d_sae=0`; PR #778).
- **Tokenizer / dataset mismatch.** Pretrained SAEs were trained on a specific tokenizer and a specific activation distribution (e.g. Pile for GPT-2, OpenWebText for Gemma Scope). Applying them to chat-template-formatted prompts gives degraded reconstruction. Sanity-check reconstruction loss on your prompts.
- **Gemma Scope 2 `*_affine` transcoders loaded wrong before 6.54.2 (no error).** Gemma Scope 2 stores `affine_skip_connection` as `(d_in, d_out)` applied as `x @ A`; SAELens's `SkipTranscoder` computes `x @ W_skip.T`, and the loader copied the matrix unchanged, so "the matrices are square, so nothing errored: every `*_affine` Gemma Scope 2 transcoder loaded fine and produced wrong output" (PR #761, fixing issue #760; released in v6.54.2, 2026-10-13). Measured in the PR (fraction of MLP-out variance explained, 16k width): 270m-pt layer 15 scored **−16.4** instead of 0.807, 1b-pt layer 7 **−3.45** instead of 0.959; per the PR, "the transposed skip connection did worse than leaving the skip connection out". Fix: `pip install -U "sae-lens>=6.54.2"`, and re-load from the original `google/gemma-scope-2-*` repo any affine transcoder you saved in SAELens format with an older version ("has the transposed matrix baked in"). Plain SAEs and non-affine transcoders were unaffected; circuit-tracer's own loader already transposes the matrix (`.T.contiguous()` in `single_layer_transcoder.py`), so only the SAELens path was wrong. Symptom: negative explained variance from an affine transcoder.
- **Saved BatchTopK SAE fires far more (or fewer) than *k* features — SAELens ≤ 6.54.8 (no error).** If you trained a `BatchTopKTrainingSAE` (or `MatryoshkaBatchTopKTrainingSAE`, which inherits it) with `normalize_activations="expected_average_only_in"` and saved it as the JumpReLU inference SAE, the saved `threshold` was in the wrong units: `SAETrainer.fit()` folds the activation-norm scaling factor into `W_enc`/`W_dec`, and with `rescale_acts_by_decoder_norm` on (the TopK training-config default) that divides the pre-activations by the factor, but `topk_threshold` was written out unchanged (issue #763, opened 2026-10-14). Per the issue, "With a scaling factor below 1 too many features fire, above 1 too few." PR #764's repro (k = 4, scaling factor 0.02) went from `mean L0 = 19.62 (training SAE: 4.00)` to `mean L0 = 4.00` after the fix, released in **v6.54.9 (2026-10-20)**; v6.54.10 fixes the same unit error for the matching-pursuit `residual_threshold` (PR #771), which also affects *loading* a pretrained matching-pursuit SAE that carries a norm scaling factor. Fix: `pip install -U "sae-lens>=6.54.10"` and re-save from a training checkpoint, or retrain; the first affected release was not established, so treat any BatchTopK SAE saved this way before 2026-10-20 as suspect. Check: the saved SAE's mean L0 on *raw* activations should equal the training *k*.
- **"CE loss recovered" (`ce_loss_score`) and `kl_div_score` were biased upward before 6.54.14 (no error).** `run_evals` excludes BOS/EOS/PAD tokens by default, and since PR #754 the SAE-spliced run leaves those positions alone, but the zero-ablation reference still zeroed *every* position, including BOS (usually the attention sink). Per PR #773: "The ablation loss is inflated, so both scores come out higher than they should." On GPT-2 small with `gpt2-small-res-jb` (4 short prompts, BOS excluded): `blocks.4.hook_resid_pre` `ce_loss_score` 0.986 → 0.976 and `kl_div_score` 0.990 → 0.983 after the fix; `blocks.8.hook_resid_pre` 0.944 → 0.926; "At layer 4 the unexplained part of the CE loss (1 − score) is under-reported by about 40% (1.4% vs 2.4%)." In the PR's two rows the relative under-reporting was larger at the better-scoring layer (about 40% at layer 4, about 25% at layer 8). Fixed in v6.54.14 (2026-11-02); recompute before comparing SAEs or citing a CE-recovered number from an older run. With nothing excluded, behaviour is unchanged. (One 4-prompt example; the size of the bias on your SAE is not known until you rerun.)
- **fp16 eval metrics overflow to `inf` / `nan` before 6.54.13.** `get_sparsity_and_variance_metrics` computed in the activation dtype: "**float16:** once a token's activation norm is above 256, its squared norm overflows 65504. `mse` comes out as `inf`, and `relative_reconstruction_bias` and `explained_variance_legacy` as `nan`" — common for real residual streams; in bfloat16 nothing overflows but `relative_reconstruction_bias` "reads 0.498 where the exact value is 0.5" (PR #775). Fixed in v6.54.13 by promoting to float32. Symptom: `mse: inf` or NaN `explained_variance_legacy` from a low-precision eval.
- **Hook point off-by-one.** Different SAEs are trained at different hook points: `blocks.{L}.hook_resid_pre` vs `hook_resid_post` vs `hook_mlp_out`. Read the SAE's metadata; loading at the wrong hook point gives you garbage features.
- **Sandwich-norm models (Gemma 2/3/4, OLMo 2/3): `hook_mlp_out` ≠ `mlp.hook_out`.** The block-level `hook_mlp_out` is the post-norm residual contribution; `mlp.hook_out` (and an HF hook on `model.model.layers[i].mlp`) is the raw MLP output. On gemma-2-2b, a Gemma Scope MLP SAE fed the raw output scored FVU 9.8 with L0 8 instead of FVU 0.26 with L0 81. Details in [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#the-sandwich-norm-hook-trap-gemma-234-olmo-23).
- **L0 vs L1 confusion.** L0 = average number of active features per token. L1 = the loss penalty that produced low L0. Modern SAEs (TopK, JumpReLU) target L0 directly; vanilla SAEs use an L1 penalty whose right value is dataset-dependent.
- **`SAE.from_pretrained("...")` not in registry** — older SAEs may need explicit `release` and `sae_id`. Check `sae_lens.loading.pretrained_saes_directory.get_pretrained_saes_directory()`.
- **Memory blowup with `HookedSAETransformer`.** Splices all SAEs at once by default; pass `use_error_term=False` and only the layers you need.
- **Inference speed.** Adding an SAE to the forward pass roughly halves throughput. For eval-time SAE feature extraction at scale, consider extracting raw activations once (vLLM-Lens) and applying SAEs offline.

**One-liner:**
```python
# pip install sae-lens
from sae_lens import SAE
sae, cfg, sparsity = SAE.from_pretrained(
    release="gemma-scope-2b-pt-res",
    sae_id="layer_20/width_16k/average_l0_71",
    device="cuda",
)
```

## EleutherAI sparsify

Aliases: `eai-sparsify` on PyPI (**new name** — the unprefixed `sparsify` on PyPI belongs to Neural Magic), `EleutherAI/sparsify` on GitHub, "EleutherAI's SAE library". Latest PyPI v1.3.3 (2026-07-16).

**What it is.** EleutherAI's SAE training library. Defaults to **TopK SAEs** (which directly enforce sparsity rather than penalizing L1) and supports transcoders via a `--transcode` flag. Experimental **GroupMax** and end-to-end training. Distributed training via `torchrun` / DDP.

**When to use it:**

- Training your own TopK SAE from scratch.
- Training a transcoder (the `--transcode` flag).
- You want fast, multi-GPU SAE training with sensible defaults (DDP, fp16/bf16).

**When *not* to use it:**

- You just want to load pretrained SAEs — use SAELens.
- You need JumpReLU / Gated / matryoshka architectures — those live in SAELens or `dictionary_learning`.
- You need crosscoders / cross-layer transcoders — see **clt-training** (a fork of sparsify).

**Pitfalls:**

- **Training bugs fixed in July 2026 — runs made with ≤1.3.1 may be affected** (the `micro_acc_steps` bug only if you set it, fixed in 1.3.2; the AuxK bug in TopK runs with an auxiliary loss, fixed in 1.3.3, so 1.3.2 still has it). v1.3.2 (2026-07-16) fixed `micro_acc_steps`, which had been a no-op since end-to-end training support was added: setting `micro_acc_steps=N` saved no memory *and* silently divided the loss by an extra factor of N, scaling gradients down (it now chunks activations for the `fvu` loss only; combining it with `ce`/`kl` losses raises). v1.3.3 (2026-07-16) fixed the TopK **AuxK loss double-counting `b_dec`**, which pulled dead latents toward `(e - b_dec)` and put an unintended gradient on `b_dec`. Symptom: unusual dead-latent counts or slow dead-latent revival in TopK runs.
- **Default optimizer changed on `main`.** A 2026-07-20 commit changed the default optimizer from `signum` to `adam` (the recipe the released SAEs used; auto learning rate for Adam is `2e-4 / sqrt(num_latents / 2**14)`). PyPI 1.3.3 predates it, so set `optimizer` explicitly when comparing runs across versions or against a `main` checkout.
- **PyPI rename.** Older docs say "install from source because PyPI sparsify is taken." That's no longer needed: `pip install eai-sparsify`. The unprefixed `sparsify` on PyPI is still Neural Magic's unrelated tool.
- **Default dataset is `EleutherAI/SmolLM2-135M-10B`.** Training on a different model? You probably want a different activation dataset; pass `--dataset`.
- **Distributed checkpoint format.** Saved SAEs use a directory layout, not a single file. Don't rename or copy without preserving the structure.

## dictionary_learning

Aliases: `dictionary_learning`, `saprmarks/dictionary_learning` on GitHub, "Sam Marks's library". Architectures: Standard, Gated, TopK, **BatchTopK** (Bussmann et al. 2024, arXiv:2412.06410 — relaxes TopK's per-token sparsity to a per-batch budget), JumpReLU, **Matryoshka BatchTopK** (Bussmann et al. 2025, arXiv:2503.17547 — trains nested dictionaries of increasing size so small dictionaries reconstruct independently, mitigating feature absorption; a real differentiator vs SAELens). Integrates with nnsight — this is how Marks-lab papers (e.g. "Sparse Feature Circuits" follow-ons) actually consume it.

**What it is.** A research-y SAE library from Sam Marks's lab. Lower-level than SAELens; closer to "here are the math primitives, build what you need." Used for many papers on transcoders and feature circuits.

**Status (2026-11-23):** research-grade and slow-cadence; expect to read source. After no commits between August 2025 and November 2026, a fix was merged on 2026-11-21 (PR #60); the only tag is still `v0.1.0`. Not abandoned, but not on the SAELens release pace.

**Pitfall — JumpReLU trainer's L0 term used post-activation values (fixed on `main` 2026-11-21, PR #60; no release).** `JumpReluTrainer.loss` computed the L0 term as a step function of the *post*-JumpReLU activation `f`; per the PR, "Below the threshold `f` is already 0", so "a pre-activation just below the threshold gives the threshold no gradient. Only features that already fired push on it, which halves the kernel window", whereas the JumpReLU paper applies the step to the pre-activation. A second effect: "with a negative threshold, `f == 0 > threshold` is true, so every inactive feature was counted in L0". The PR notes that for a positive threshold the forward value of L0 is unchanged; what changes is the threshold's gradient, so in JumpReLU SAEs trained with this library before the fix, the L0 term pushed on a threshold only through features that had already fired. The size of the effect on trained dictionaries was not measured in the PR. If you compare JumpReLU SAEs trained here against other libraries, record the commit.

**When to use it:** You're doing methodological research on SAE architectures (new loss functions, new sparsity mechanisms, Matryoshka-style hierarchical SAEs) and want minimal abstraction. You're working in the Marks-lab feature-circuits idiom (nnsight + sparse-feature attribution).

**When *not* to use it:** You want plug-and-play; SAELens is friendlier. You expect frequent updates.

## Neuronpedia

Aliases: `neuronpedia.org`, "the SAE feature browser", `hijohnnylin/neuronpedia` on GitHub (open source, Johnny Lin / Decode Research; latest release v2.23.4 on 2026-11-21 — thirteen minor versions since v2.10.1 of 2026-11-03).

**What it is.** A web UI and API for browsing, searching, and steering with SAE features. Hosts auto-interp explanations, activation visualizations, and per-feature dashboards for many open SAE releases (Gemma Scope, Gemma Scope 2, Llama Scope, GPT-2 small, etc.). It is also the hosting layer for several newer interpretability tools: **attribution graphs** from circuit-tracer (create or upload graphs; the model must exist on Neuronpedia), the **Jacobian-lens family** at `neuronpedia.org/jlens` (J-Lens, J++ Lens and Oracle Lens readouts; lens weights in the `neuronpedia/jacobian-lens` HuggingFace repo — see [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#workspace-lenses-j-lens-r-lens-j-lens)), and **natural language autoencoders** at `neuronpedia.org/nla`.

**Neurons, not only SAE features (v2.8.0, 2026-10-20).** Release notes: "neuron sources with dashboards in files", neuron search-all ranked "by both tails of each neuron's distribution" (so a neuron's *negative* pole is searchable, not only its top positive activations) and by per-neuron percentile, and the NLA verbalizer moved to interp-engine 1.13.2. Useful if you want to compare a dictionary feature against a raw MLP-neuron baseline (see "Should I use SAEs at all?" above). **v2.9.0 (2026-10-27)** adds steering of raw MLP neurons on dashboard models with inference ("feat: steer MLP neurons on dashboard models with inference"), so a neuron steering baseline can sit next to an SAE-feature steer in the same UI; **v2.10.0 (2026-11-02)** makes the Qwen 3.6 27B J-lens tab show model, lens and deployment info on hover. The inference server moved to interp-engine 1.14.3 in v2.8.5 (2026-10-27). **November 2026 releases (release notes' commit lines):** R-Lens fits converted for hosting (v2.11.0, 2026-11-05); explanations from external sites "with credit and an upload API" (v2.12.0); side-by-side comparison of two feature dashboards (v2.15.0); a `/neurons` release page, with MLP neurons tagged as a community release and the page defaulting to `qwen3-8b` (v2.17.0–v2.19.0, 2026-11-10); and J-Lens, J++ and R-Lens token lists built "for ICA components and MLP neurons" (v2.21.0). So a neuron's or ICA component's lens readout can now be browsed next to SAE features.

**An independent neuron and attention-head catalog: `stdlogits/neuron-lens`** (GitHub, MIT; `neuron-lens.com`). Every MLP neuron and attention head of Qwen3-8B and Gemma 4 12B read from weights through two Jacobian lenses, from behaviour on 15M tokens, and from token drivers, with the rest of the Qwen3 family up to 14B catalogued the same way (README; community work, labels not evaluated here). Details in [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#how-do-i-read-an-mlp-neuron-through-a-lens-neuron-output-vectors-j-share).

**Community neuron map on a laptop-sized model: `moridinamael/bonsai-interp`** (MIT; moridinamael, *Twitch Plays: AI Interpretability of Bonsai-8B*, LessWrong, 2026-10-19). An open, pull-request-driven map of the 36 layers × 12,288 = 442,368 MLP neurons of Bonsai-8B, PrismML's 1-bit model derived from Qwen3-8B (about 1.15 GB on disk). Per the post, "Every test is committed before it is run. A label holds only if its sentences beat matched near-misses, and GitHub independently re-scores every submitted result before merging"; missions need "A GPU with 8 GB+ or an Apple Silicon Mac". **When not to use it:** as a source of vetted labels — they are unreviewed community work on a 1-bit quantized model, whose neurons need not match full-precision Qwen3-8B.

**When to use it:**

- Looking up "what does feature 3217 in Gemma-2-2B layer 20 do?"
- Searching for features by natural-language description.
- Sharing SAE results with collaborators (deep links to features).
- Pulling auto-interp explanations programmatically (Neuronpedia API).

**When *not* to use it:** Your SAE isn't on Neuronpedia (you trained it yourself). You can host private dashboards via SAEDashboard / SAEVis.

## Delphi (EleutherAI)

Aliases: `delphi`, `EleutherAI/delphi` on GitHub, `eai-delphi` on PyPI. Latest **v0.2.0 (2026-11-02)**, after **v0.1.4** the same day — the first releases since v0.1.3 (2026-03-12); both are on PyPI as `eai-delphi`. `eai-delphi` 0.2.0 depends on `eai-sparsify`, `vllm>=0.10.2` and `httpx>=0.28` (PyPI metadata). The codebase behind Paulo et al. 2024, "Automatically Interpreting Millions of Features" (arXiv:2410.13928).

**What it is.** A library for **automated interpretability** of SAE features and transcoder latents — generates and scores natural-language explanations using an explainer LLM. Scoring methods include **detection**, **recall**, and **fuzzing** for evaluating explanation quality. Pairs with sparsify; works with SAELens.

**When to use it:** You trained your own SAE and want to auto-generate explanations for the features. You want detection / recall / fuzz scoring of explanation quality.

**vs alternatives:** Use **Delphi** if your SAE is local (you trained it). Use **Neuronpedia auto-interp** if your SAE is already hosted on Neuronpedia — easier path, no infra.

**Pitfalls:**

- **Auto-interp quality scales with the explainer model.** Cheap explainer models (GPT-3.5, small open models) produce vague explanations. Budget for a strong model.
- **`pip install delphi` installs the wrong package.** The PyPI name `delphi` belongs to an unrelated ML4AI project ("A framework for assembling probabilistic models from text", last release 2018). EleutherAI's Delphi is `pip install "eai-delphi>=0.1.4"` (0.2.0 is current).
- **Detection and fuzzing scores before v0.1.4 are inflated by train/test overlap (no error).** With the default sampler config, the explainer's examples and the scorers' test examples were drawn from the same records by `split_quantiles`, which reseeds the global RNG with the same seed. Per the v0.1.4 release notes: "in quantile 0 random.sample(q, 4) and random.sample(q, 5) make the same first 4 picks: 4 of the 5 top-quantile test examples are always train examples. In total 10-20 of the 50 default test examples had been shown to the explainer, which inflates detection and fuzzing scores." v0.1.4 samples test examples only from those not shown to the explainer. Re-score explanations before comparing old Delphi numbers with new ones (the 10–20 of 50 figure is the maintainers'; the size of the score inflation was not reported).
- **Simulator correlations were NaN, not bad explanations (before v0.1.4).** On transformers ≥5, `apply_chat_template` returns a `BatchEncoding`, and BOS-less tokenizers such as Qwen2.5 broke the tab-token lookup; both raised `IndexError`, and "simulate() catches the exception and returns all-zero predictions, so every simulation correlation became NaN without an error."
- **Other v0.1.4 fixes (some changed numbers silently, some fixed crashes):** the surprisal scorer reused a mutated KV cache, so "later batches attended to earlier batches' sentences and got wrong losses"; result analysis with several hookpoints globbed `*{module}*`, so "with hookpoints 'layers.1' and 'layers.10' the score files of layers.10 were also loaded, and counted, as layers.1" (and every module after the first raised `KeyError: 0`); `IntruderScorer(type="internal")` crashed on "every batch whose quantile is not the first one"; fuzzing mark distributions were matched between labels; `failed_count` was always zero.
- **`--n_examples_test 5` silently produced no scoring examples before v0.2.0.** With the default `--n_quantiles 10`, `split_quantiles` took `n_samples // n_quantiles` = 0 per quantile, "with no warning". v0.2.0 spreads the remainder across quantiles (default configs that divided evenly are unchanged). v0.2.0 also adds `--server-port` to use an external vLLM / OpenAI-compatible server instead of loading the explainer in-process, and wires validated explanation search into the CLI.
- **On transformers ≥5, token highlighting silently disappeared in v0.1.3 (fixed in v0.1.4).** `batch_decode` on a 1-D token tensor returns one joined string on transformers 5, so "token highlighting silently disappears from explainer and fuzz/detection prompts, the simulator's `ActivationRecord` gets a length-1 token list, and the intruder scorer raises `IndexError`" (PR #177, fixing #176). transformers is unpinned, so a fresh install of `eai-delphi` 0.1.3 hits this. Searchable symptom: `IndexError` from the intruder scorer, or explainer prompts with no highlighted tokens.
- **Explanation parsing took the *first* `[EXPLANATION]:` marker** (PR #179; released in v0.1.4): a chain-of-thought explainer returned its whole reasoning chain as the label, and top-activating subject-model text containing the marker could inject a label that "flows verbatim through `ExplainerResult.explanation` into `DetectionScorer` / `IntruderScorer` / `OpenAISimulator` and into operator-facing auto-interp tooling". The release notes flag a residual case: an injected marker on the literal last line still wins.
- **Check before a large run:** that an explainer prompt shows highlighted tokens, that simulation correlations are not NaN, and that you are on `eai-delphi>=0.1.4`.
- **An alternative scorer:** Transluce's released simulator model scores any feature explanation by correlation with true activations (Llama-3.1-8B features only; see [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#automated-interpretability-and-interpretability-agents)).

## clt-training (EleutherAI)

Aliases: `clt-training`, `EleutherAI/clt-training` on GitHub, "cross-layer transcoders". **Fork of `EleutherAI/sparsify`** — sparsify configs partly transfer. Adds DTensor tensor parallelism, bfloat16, KL+FVU training, pre-layernorm hookpoints.

## circuit-tracer

Aliases: `circuit-tracer`, **`decoderesearch/circuit-tracer` on GitHub** (canonical; `safety-research/circuit-tracer` is the old slug and still resolves to it), "the Anthropic Transformer Circuits team's transcoder circuit library", "attribution graphs library". Authors Michael Hanna, Mateusz Piotrowski, Jack Lindsey, Emmanuel Ameisen; maintained by Decode Research (`decoderesearch`). Latest tag and PyPI release **v0.5.4 (2026-11-22)** — the first PyPI release since 0.5.0 (2026-03-29); its only change over v0.5.3 (2026-10-14) is a workflow that publishes releases to PyPI, so `pip install circuit-tracer` now carries the v0.5.3 fixes. PyPI 0.5.4 metadata: `nnsight>=0.8.0rc1,<0.9`, `transformer-lens>=2.16.0` (no upper bound), `transformers>=4.56.0,<=4.57.3`, `huggingface-hub<1.0.0`. **On `main` after 0.5.4 (not released as of 2026-11-23):** transformers 5 and `huggingface_hub>=1.0.0` support (PR #79, merged 2026-11-23; `pyproject.toml` on `main` reads `transformer-lens>=2.16.0,<4`, `transformers>=4.56.0`), and a fix for top-k transcoders losing their activation function on the cache / local-folder loading path (PR #129; pitfall below). Install `main` from a clone (`pip install .`) or `pip install "git+https://github.com/decoderesearch/circuit-tracer.git"` if you need either. v0.5.3 brought the nnsight-0.8 backend into a tagged release ("NNsight backend bumped to 0.8", #113) and fixes three silent correctness bugs (below). An interp-engine backend is proposed (PR #111, "feat: interp-engine support", open since 2026-08-21).

**What it is.** A library for **circuit-level analysis on top of MLP transcoder features** — given trained transcoders, it discovers attribution graphs (which features at which layers drove a particular completion), renders them, and supports interventions on features. The transcoder-circuit approach was introduced by Dunefsky, Chlenski & Nanda 2024, "Transcoders Find Interpretable LLM Feature Circuits" (arXiv:2406.11944; NeurIPS 2024); circuit-tracer implements the attribution-graph methods from Anthropic's Transformer Circuits team (Ameisen et al. 2025, Lindsey et al. 2025). It runs three steps — attribution, graph-file creation (pruning), local visualization server (`circuit-tracer attribute --prompt ... --transcoder_set gemma --slug ... --graph_file_dir ... --server`) — and graphs can also be created on Neuronpedia with no install.

**Available transcoder sets (from the README, 2026-10):** Gemma-2 2B per-layer transcoders (PLTs) and cross-layer transcoders (CLTs; 426K and 2.5M features); Llama-3.2 1B PLTs and CLTs; Qwen-3 PLTs (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B); a GPT-OSS 20B CLT (`mntss/clt-131k`); **Gemma-3 PLTs from Gemma Scope 2 for 270M, 1B, 4B, 12B and 27B (pretrained and instruction-tuned; these require the `nnsight` backend)**; and **Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct TopK PLTs** (`facebook/crv-8b-instruct-transcoders`; license `cc-by-nc-4.0`, i.e. non-commercial). Locally trained transcoders load by passing their full root path. Added in v0.5.0 (April 2026): support for TopK transcoders and local CLT features in graphs. The `lazy_encoder` / `lazy_decoder` options need transcoders in `circuit-tracer`-compatible format; the README documents `save_transcoders_to_cache` for converting them.

**Third-party set at 70B instruct scale: `FraunhoferHHI/llama-3.3-70b-instruct-transcoders`** (Hugging Face, created 2026-11-04; license `llama3.3`; `library_name: circuit-tracer`). Per-layer skip transcoders for all 80 MLP layers of Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, trained as BatchTopK and exported with per-feature JumpReLU thresholds; d_transcoder 65,536 (8× expansion); reads `mlp.hook_in` (post-attention-layernorm output) and predicts `mlp.hook_out` (MLP output before the residual add); bf16, about 171 GB for the 80 layer files. Load with `ReplacementModel.from_pretrained("meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct", "FraunhoferHHI/llama-3.3-70b-instruct-transcoders", backend="nnsight", dtype=torch.bfloat16)`; the card says attribution "needs the weights sharded over several GPUs". The publisher's own evaluation (FVU = fraction of variance unexplained; CE-recovered = share of the cross-entropy increase from ablating the MLP that the transcoder restores): "Headline, across all 80 layers: **FVU mean 0.506** (min 0.030, max 0.657), **L0 ≈ 29.5**, never-fired features 11.5% mean"; median CE-recovered 0.91 on the 7 layers whose MLP changes CE by ≥ 0.1 nats; "**FVU is 0.5–0.65 through the mid-stack.** This is an 8× expansion trained on 1.75B tokens/layer … usable but under-trained" relative to published 1–2B-model skip transcoders (FVU 0.16–0.33 at 28–32× expansion); layers 74–77 are 59–79% never-fired. **When not to use it:** without checking the error-node share of each graph — at FVU ≈ 0.5 mid-stack, much of each MLP's output is left to error nodes; on fewer GPUs than the 70B model plus ~171 GB of transcoders need; as a reviewed artifact (released 2026-11-04; the evaluations are the publisher's).

**When to use it:** You've trained or loaded transcoders (via sparsify `--transcode`, dictionary_learning, CLT-Forge, or pretrained sets above) and you want to do circuit discovery — "which features explain this output?" — rather than just feature inspection. The natural next step after most SAE/transcoder projects.

**When *not* to use it:** You only have residual-stream SAEs (not transcoders) and just want feature dashboards — SAEDashboard or Neuronpedia is the right tool. You need attention-mechanism explanations — QK attributions and head loadings were requested (issue #53) and proposed (#114) but are not in a release.

**Automating the manual step.** Grouping features into *supernodes* is normally done by hand. Patel, Zhang & Hu, *LLMs Can Annotate Attribution Graphs* (arXiv:2608.02632, 2026-07-28; ICML 2026 Mechanistic Interpretability Workshop; code `maxh119Z/circuit-tracer-automation`, data `circuit-tracer-automation/pipeline_automation` on HuggingFace) feed feature descriptions (from top-activating text on Neuronpedia plus logit effects) to a language model that groups them into supernodes; on Gemma-2-2B with Gemma Scope transcoders the supernodes scored as interpretable as the 15 human-annotated reference graphs, and the pipeline recovered the intermediate-hop supernode on a two-hop "Capitals" task in 97 of 100 prompts. Limits stated by the authors: one model, one transcoder suite, one annotator LLM (GPT-5 mini), a naive grouping that ignores layer/position and attribution flow, and supernode labels too coarse for arithmetic circuits. Use it to triage many graphs, not to replace reading the graph for a claim you will publish.

**Pitfalls:**

- **Attribution graphs are computationally heavy on long prompts.** Start with short prompts and small token windows; the renderer can choke on dense graphs. A long-context OOM on an 80 GB H100 at ~6,000-token prompts is an open issue (#92); a long-context memory estimator is proposed (#109).
- **`ImportError: cannot import name 'HookedTransformer' from 'transformer_lens'` on `import circuit_tracer`** (issue #115, closed 2026-11-23). The default backend's `ReplacementModel` subclasses TransformerLens' `HookedTransformer`, which TransformerLens 4.0 (2026-09-21) removed; the released packages declare `transformer-lens>=2.16.0` with no upper bound. Two statements about the current release that do not fully agree, both reported as found: the maintainers' closing comment says the bound `transformer-lens>=2.16.0,<4` is on `main` and "The latest release (v0.5.4) predates that, so installs from PyPI keep hitting this until the next release carries the bound"; but the 0.5.4 metadata also caps `transformers<=4.57.3`, while TransformerLens 4.x requires `transformers>=5.9.0` (PyPI metadata), so a clean resolve of `circuit-tracer==0.5.4` cannot select TransformerLens 4 and lands on a 3.x release — the error then arises only when TransformerLens 4 is already forced into the environment by something else, or when the install ignores the transformers cap. The safe move either way: pin `transformer-lens<4` explicitly, or use `ReplacementModel.from_pretrained(model_name, backend='nnsight')` (experimental: slower and more memory-hungry, per the README). Because 0.5.4 needs transformers 4, it cannot share an environment with tools that need transformers 5 (TransformerLens 4, nnterp 2, current vLLM) — use a separate environment or `main`.
- **Top-k transcoder sets loaded from the cache or a local folder ran with ReLU, not top-k — circuit-tracer ≤ 0.5.4 (fixed on `main`, PR #129, merged 2026-11-22; first affected version not stated).** "Top-k transcoder sets lost their activation function on the cache / local-folder loading path": "`save_transcoders_to_cache("facebook/crv-8b-instruct-transcoders")` followed by a load gave ReLU, as did `ReplacementModel.from_pretrained(model, "/abs/path")` on a local top-k folder." Loading straight from the Hub already passed the activation through. Attribution graphs built from top-k transcoders (the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct `crv-8b` set, or your own TopK transcoders in a local folder) via those two paths used the wrong activation function (ReLU keeps every positive pre-activation, not the top k); rebuild them from `main`. ReLU and JumpReLU sets were unaffected.
- **nnsight version coupling.** PyPI 0.5.4, the v0.5.3 tag and `main` all require `nnsight>=0.8.0rc1,<0.9` (the nnsight backend was ported to 0.8 on 2026-09-11). nnsight 0.8 has been the stable PyPI release since 0.8.0 (2026-10-16; current 0.8.4, 2026-11-23) and breaks 0.7 code (see [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#nnsight)); an environment pinned to `nnsight<0.8` for other work can raise dependency conflicts. Whether nnsight 0.8.4 runs correctly on the transformers 4.57 that circuit-tracer 0.5.4 requires was not tested.
- **Compare graphs against a neuron baseline.** Shabalin & Piotrowski (arXiv:2610.24521; Piotrowski is a circuit-tracer author) find that many transcoder features are near-copies of single MLP neurons and that sparsified neurons, given a trained linear output map, beat transcoders on downstream KL; Transluce's `TransluceAI/circuits` traces circuits directly on MLP neurons (arXiv:2601.22594). When a graph's story rests on a few features, check whether the same story holds in the neuron basis (details in "Should I use SAEs at all?" above).
- **CLT skip weights were dropped on save in ≤0.5.2 (fixed in v0.5.3, PR #117).** "Saving and reloading an affine CLT silently drops `W_skip`, so the reloaded model reconstructs different activations" (in the PR's small CPU reproduction a reconstructed value went from 20.5 to 8.5). The PR notes that "weights already discarded by older saves cannot be recovered": re-create such checkpoints from the original training run or source weights with ≥0.5.3, rather than re-saving the broken file.
- **Inactive trailing token positions were dropped in sparse CLT reconstruction (fixed in v0.5.3, PR #118).** Reconstruction inferred the token count from the last active feature, so it dropped trailing inactive tokens, crashed when no features survived the BOS mask, and "could silently broadcast a one-token reconstruction across later error vectors".
- **`prune_graph` kept zero-weight edges at `edge_threshold=1.0`** before v0.5.3 (PR #116).
- **Graphs over TopK SAEs are "decided at the margin": recompute which features are active after an intervention.** Karl Li, "Sparse Autoencoder Attribution Graphs Are Decided at the Margin" (arXiv:2611.00606, 2026-10-31; single author). On five public TopK SAE pairs from three Pythia models (largest pythia-160m), "zeroing one feature moves a next layer feature across the cutoff in 0.74 of cases or more, and the crossings carry most of the response's energy"; over writes that scale a feature's code by three factors, "the graph built on the features active beforehand has a median relative error of 0.247 or more", while a **margin graph** that "adds the first order change to every preactivation and reranks, at the same cost" errs by at most 0.033. Between training seeds of one testbed, sparse maps between features explain 0.85 of a graph's energy on its active features but 0.47 once the other seed's graph keeps only its own. Scope: TopK SAEs on small Pythia models; whether the effect carries over to circuit-tracer's JumpReLU transcoders is untested (a JumpReLU threshold is also a cutoff, so check). Practical rule: when you use a graph to *predict* an ablation or steer, re-run the encoder after the edit rather than trusting the pre-edit active set.
- **Error-node ids changed in v0.5.1** (2026-07-12): error nodes are now `"{layer}_-1_{pos}"` instead of `"0_{layer}_{pos}"`, which previously collided with layer-0 transcoder feature ids. Graph files or analysis code written against the old id format misparse error nodes.
- **Replacement-model faithfulness.** Splicing SAEs or transcoders into several layers at once yields a model whose outputs can be badly degraded, which is why graphs carry *error nodes* for the unexplained residual. A 2026 blog post (Evan Lloyd, LessWrong 2026-07-27, unreviewed; code in `evan-lloyd/mechinterp-experiments`) argues for *replacement-aware training* — a loss term penalising distortion of the next layer's features — on Gemma-2-2B residual SAEs instead of relying on error nodes. Treat error-node mass as the first number to report with any graph.

## Lorsa (Low-Rank Sparse Attention)

Aliases: "Lorsa", "low-rank sparse attention", "attention SAE alternative", "attention superposition", He et al. 2025; code `OpenMOSS/Lorsa` (a pointer repo) → implementation in **`OpenMOSS/Llamascopium`** (the `OpenMOSS/Language-Model-SAEs` URL redirects there; verified, last push 2026-10-04), PyPI `llamascopium` (latest 2.0.0b34, a pre-release; Python ≥3.11, <3.13).

**What it is.** He, Wang, Lin, Ge, Shu, Tang, Zhang & Qiu, *Towards Understanding the Nature of Attention with Low-Rank Sparse Decomposition* (arXiv:2504.20938; ICLR 2026 per the Llamascopium README). A sparse *replacement model* for multi-head self-attention (MHSA): many low-rank attention heads of which only a few are active per token, trained to reproduce the attention layer's output. Motivation is *attention superposition* — behaviour computed collectively by several heads, so no single original head is interpretable. Reported results: Lorsa heads recover cleaner, finer-grained versions of induction heads, successor heads and attention-sink behaviour; a family of arithmetic-specific Lorsa heads in Llama-3.1-8B, each corresponding to one atomic operation; automated-interpretability scores at parity with SAEs, and "superior circuit discovery properties, especially for features computed collectively by multiple MHSA heads". The Llamascopium README (2026-02-12 entry) adds *Complete Replacement Models*, which combine transcoders and Lorsas to sparsify a whole model (blog post, not checked here).

**When to use it:** your circuit question runs through attention — which earlier-position feature is being moved, and why — where MLP transcoders and circuit-tracer's graphs say little (circuit-tracer has no QK attribution in a release; see above).

**A QK-side dashboard to browse first (GPT-2 small only).** nuppal, "Opening up Attention: A QK Transcoder Dashboard for Feature Pairs" (LessWrong, 2026-10-22): query-key transcoders (separate sparse query-side and key-side dictionaries replacing the QK computation, in the spirit of Wynroe and Sharkey's bilinear sparse dictionary learning) with an interactive feature-pair dashboard for every layer and head of GPT-2 small at `nuppal-qk-feature-dashboard.hf.space`. Single-author post; use it to explore attention-pattern formation, not as a validated method.

**When *not* to use it:** you want pretrained artifacts and mature tooling on your model — Lorsa needs training per model and layer, the pip package is a beta pre-release, and circuit-tracer's README does not mention Lorsa (2026-10-12), so attribution graphs that include Lorsa heads mean working inside Llamascopium.

## Qwen3 dictionaries from ByteDance Seed (Cross-Layer TopK CLT, MoE-output SAEs)

Aliases: Cross-Layer TopK, cross-layer transcoder (CLT) for Qwen3-8B-Base, `ByteDance-Seed/Cross-Layer-TopK-CLT`, "SAE Interprets MoE", `ByteDance-Seed/Qwen3-30B-A3B-MoE-SAE`, `ByteDance-Seed/SAE-interprets-MoE`, mixture-of-experts SAE, expert specialization.

**Cross-Layer TopK CLT (Qwen3-8B-Base).** Zhuo, Zhang, Li, Lin, Gu, Wang & Lu (ByteDance Seed), "Cross-Layer TopK: Dynamically Allocating Sparsity Across Layers in Transcoders" (arXiv:2611.09233; preprint, PDF dated 2026-11-09). A cross-layer transcoder (CLT) reads the residual stream before each layer's MLP and writes to the MLP outputs of that layer and every later one. Instead of a per-layer TopK or a JumpReLU penalty, the method "sets a single L0 budget for the whole model and dynamically allocates it across layers during training", because intermediate layers are harder to reconstruct. Reported on Qwen3-8B-Base against JumpReLU and other TopK variants: "lower FVU while using 20% fewer active latents than JumpReLU" (FVU = fraction of variance unexplained); higher replacement and completeness scores on attribution graphs averaged over 100 prompts (at the same budget of 360 latents per token, +0.04 replacement and +0.008 completeness over per-layer TopK); and, with sparse decoding, "a 2.9× speedup per training step". These are the authors' numbers on one model.

- **Weights:** `ByteDance-Seed/Cross-Layer-TopK-CLT` on Hugging Face (Apache-2.0; created 2026-11-05). Model card: "36 layers × 32,768 latents", "94.2B parameters, fp32, 377 GB in total" (357 GB of that is decoders; encode-only runs fit one 80 GB GPU). Encoding keeps "the `36 · k = 360` highest-scoring latents across all layers jointly", scored by activation times decoder norm. Hooks: `resid_mid` is the input of `model.layers[l].post_attention_layernorm`; `mlp_out` is the output of `model.layers[l].mlp`. Two instructions from the card that are easy to miss: "The CLT was trained on fp32 activations; use fp32 for the base model too", and tokens with outlier norms are dropped as in training ("in practice the first token").
- **Not a circuit-tracer drop-in (as far as the card shows).** The card documents its own safetensors layout and a standalone `torchrun` demo; it declares no `library_name` and does not mention `circuit-tracer`, `clt-training` or SAELens. Using it for attribution graphs in circuit-tracer would need a format conversion *and* support for a top-k taken jointly across layers; whether that works was not tested. The paper computed its own replacement and completeness scores.
- **When to use it:** you want a pretrained CLT on a current Qwen base model for feature or circuit work and can write your own loading code; you are training CLTs and want an alternative to the JumpReLU recipe. **When *not* to use it:** you need Neuronpedia or circuit-tracer integration today; you work on the instruction-tuned Qwen3-8B (this is the base model); you lack ~377 GB of disk for the decoders.

**MoE-output SAEs (Qwen3-30B-A3B).** Zhuo, Zhang, Lin & Lu (ByteDance Seed), "SAE Interprets MoE: Sparse AutoEncoders Reveal That MoE Experts Exhibit Human-Interpretable Fine-Grained Specialization" (arXiv:2611.08089; PDF dated 2026-11-06; code `ByteDance-Seed/SAE-interprets-MoE`, Apache-2.0). They train a sparse autoencoder (SAE) on the *output* of a mixture-of-experts (MoE) layer; because that output is a router-weighted sum of expert outputs and the SAE encoder is affine, "the pre-activation of every latent decomposes exactly over the experts", which attributes each latent to the experts that drive it. On layers 4, 30 and 46, "contribution is far more concentrated than gating, most latents have only one or two dominators". Weights: `ByteDance-Seed/Qwen3-30B-A3B-MoE-SAE` (Apache-2.0) — per the card, "TopK sparse autoencoders (k = 100, 2^20 latents)" at layers 4, 30 and 46, about 17.2 GB each, reading the output of `model.model.layers[L].mlp` (the MoE block, before the residual addition) "as is, without any normalization". **When to use it:** questions about what individual experts do in an MoE model. **When *not* to use it:** as a general residual-stream SAE suite (three layers, MoE-block output only); through SAELens without writing a loader (own format).

## Gemma Scope 2

Aliases: `google/gemma-scope-2` (landing page), `google/gemma-scope-2-{270m,1b,4b,12b,27b}-{pt,it}` on HuggingFace, "GemmaScope-2", Google DeepMind. Released December 2025 (HF landing page created 2025-12-15), CC-BY-4.0, loads through SAELens (`library_name: saelens`). Interactive browser: `neuronpedia.org/gemma-scope-2`. Technical report: PDF linked from the HuggingFace landing page and the Google DeepMind blog post.

**What it is.** The successor to Gemma Scope (which covers Gemma 2): an open suite of **Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) and transcoders for the Gemma 3 family** at 270M, 1B, 4B, 12B and 27B parameters, both pretrained and instruction-tuned. Per the model card: SAEs at three sites (`resid_post`, `attn_out`, `mlp_out`) and transcoders (including skip-transcoders) at four depths (25%, 50%, 65%, 85%) in the "subset" folders, and at *every* layer in the `*_all` folders (a smaller width/L0 range); plus multi-layer models — weakly causal `crosscoder` (4 concatenated residual-stream layers; the landing page says these cover every base Gemma 3 model) and `clt` (cross-layer transcoders reconstructing the whole model's MLP outputs; **only the 270M and 1B repos have a `clt` folder** — the 4B repo does not). ## Parameter decomposition (SPD, VPD) — weight-space alternative to SAEs

Aliases: "parameter decomposition", "APD" (Attribution-based Parameter Decomposition, Braun, Bushnaq, Heimersheim, Mendel & Sharkey, arXiv:2501.14926, January 2025 — the first version; toy settings only, and the authors note scaling to non-toy models remains a challenge), "SPD" (Stochastic Parameter Decomposition, Bushnaq, Braun & Sharkey, arXiv:2506.20790, June 2025), "VPD" (adVersarial Parameter Decomposition; Goodfire, *Interpreting Language Model Parameters*, 2026-05-05, https://www.goodfire.com/research/interpreting-lm-parameters; now also on arXiv as Bushnaq, Braun, Clive-Griffin, Bussmann, Hu, Ivanitskiy, Linsefors & Sharkey, arXiv:2610.20748, 2026-10-14, 124 pages), `goodfire-ai/param-decomp` on GitHub (MIT; verified, last push 2026-11-06, with a new `public/2026-11-06` tag whose changes were not read).

**What it is.** Instead of decomposing *activations* into a dictionary (SAEs, transcoders, crosscoders), parameter decomposition decomposes a model's **weight matrices** into many rank-one "subcomponents" that sum back to the original parameters, are sparse (any one input token needs few of them) and are trained to be *mechanistically faithful* (ablating the unneeded ones leaves the output unchanged). VPD's change from SPD is training against adversarially chosen ablations instead of stochastic ones. Goodfire reports less feature splitting than transcoders and that it applies to attention layers too. The repository includes a handbook (`docs/handbook.md`), a compact `nano_param_decomp/` implementation, and JAX training configs.

**When to use it:** You are doing methods research on faithful, weight-space decompositions, or want a decomposition that explains a computation in the *network's own parameters* rather than in a learned activation basis.

**When *not* to use it:** You want to interpret a production-scale model — the paper's decomposition is of a **4-layer, ~67M-parameter language model** (38,912 subcomponents, ~10,000 alive); the reference config in the repo needs a 32-GPU mesh (`fsdp: 32`), Blackwell GPUs need the `cuda13` extra and driver r580+, and no pretrained decompositions of 1B+ models were found (2026-10). For "find the feature for a concept I can name" use a probe or SAE.

**Known limits (reported):**

- Goodfire's own numbers: validation cross-entropy 2.72 with unmasked subcomponents vs 2.71 for the target; Pareto-dominates transcoders on tested sparsity measures *but the advantage disappears under matched training objectives*; adversarial robustness is limited (KL divergence 0.83 after 20 adversarial steps, 25.26 after 160).
- An independent audit (Tom Angsten, LessWrong 2026-09-30; code `angsten/vpd-audit`, verified) of the *released* decomposition found that aggregating the components needed by many inputs makes the model's output drift away from the original — at 64 tokens' worth of components (~4,500) with no search, KL ≈ 0.80 nats, comparable to the paper's own 20-step adversary (0.83 nats); deleting all components never labelled "needed" moved the model 1.28 nats. It audits the paper's decomposition, not the newer unpublished recipe in the repo. Do not assume "removing these components only affects this behaviour".
- A second audit by the same author (Tom Angsten, *VPD's Evaluation Adversary Finds Systematic Defects in the Released Decomposition*, LessWrong, 2026-10-20; code `angsten/vpd-audit-2`, MIT) ran the paper's own evaluation adversary on batches of 128 texts (80 searches) and applied each mask it found, unchanged, to 16 unseen batches. Counting only KL above the 0.22 nats a random mask already causes, a mask "keeps 78% of its divergence at 20 steps (95% interval: 68% to 88%), 82% at 40, 92% at 80, and about 95% at 160 and 320"; a shuffled control (same number of components switched off, chosen at random) shows the damage comes from *which* components are masked. So the large adversarial KL in the paper's table (0.83 nats at 20 steps, 40.2 at 320, per the audit) reflects systematic faithfulness defects of this decomposition, not an adversary exploiting input-specific interference noise. Scope (the author's): one released decomposition of one small model; the VPD authors report a newer, more robust decomposition of the same model that is not public, so it could not be audited.

## Weight-sparse transformers (interpretable by construction)

Aliases: "weight-sparse transformers", "circuit sparsity", "sparse circuits", "intrinsically interpretable models", "interpretable by design", Gao et al. 2025, `openai/circuit_sparsity` on GitHub (Apache-2.0; verified, last push 2026-09-04).

**What it is.** Instead of decomposing a dense model after the fact (SAEs, transcoders, parameter decomposition), train a transformer in which most weights are constrained to zero, so each neuron has only a few connections, then **prune** it on a narrow task to isolate the responsible circuit. Gao, Rajaram, Coxon, Govande, Baker & Mossing (OpenAI), *Weight-sparse transformers have interpretable circuits*, arXiv:2511.13653 (2025-11-17): the pruned circuits often contain neurons and residual channels corresponding to natural concepts with a few straightforwardly interpretable connections; sparser weights trade capability for interpretability, and scaling model size improves that frontier. The authors' own limit: "scaling sparse models beyond tens of millions of nonzero parameters while preserving interpretability remains a challenge." They also report preliminary results suggesting the method can be adapted to explain existing dense models. The repo provides a `GPT` inference implementation (`circuit_sparsity.inference.gpt.load_model`), released models and pruning sweeps hosted on `openaipublic` blob storage, and a Streamlit circuit visualizer (`streamlit run circuit_sparsity/viz.py`); install with `pip install -e .` from a clone. It also ships a minimal training script, `circuit_sparsity/train.py`, whose docstring says it "can train the models from the paper, albeit slowly". For the pruning algorithm itself, jacob_drori's replication (below) describes its reimplementation, `jacobcd52/sparse_pretrain_public` (small, no license file), as additionally implementing pruning, bridges training and multi-GPU support relative to the OpenAI release.

**Faithfulness caveat (read before building on it).** jacob_drori, *Weight-Sparse Circuits May Be Interpretable Yet Unfaithful* (LessWrong, 2026-02-09; Anthropic Fellows work) replicated the main result on two-layer models trained on SimpleStories, then found that the pruning step can produce circuits that do not reflect the model's computation: pruning reached task loss below 0.05 with about 30 nodes on a **nonsense task** (male name → "is", female name → "when"); a pruned circuit solved a pronoun task with *uniform* attention where the original head attended to the name; nodes took on different roles in the pruned model than in the original; and on simplified IOI (indirect object identification) pruned only on opposite-gender prompts, many seeds found a circuit that fails on **same-gender prompts**, although the original model handled them (81% correct). The author's scope note: the results "only show that the pruning algorithm is flawed", not that weight-sparse training is. A related design point from the post: switching from mean to zero ablation shrank circuits but may have made them less faithful.

**When to use it:** methods research on interpretable-by-construction models; small-model studies where you want circuits a person can read in a day (Leo Gao's *An Ambitious Vision for Interpretability* frames this as the route to "an interpretable GPT-1"; see [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#agenda-framing-pragmatic-ambitious-and-open-problems)). Close relatives: parameter decomposition (above); sparse *fine-tuning* adapters such as TopKLoRA, which make a finetune's own changes interpretable by construction (see [`model-diffing.md`](model-diffing.md#interpretable-by-construction-finetunes-topklora)); and *tensor/bilinear transformers* — Logan Riggs argues (LessWrong opinion post, 2026-09-02) that anyone interpreting models under 1B parameters should use bilinear MLP and attention variants so the whole network is analysable with linear algebra.

**When *not* to use it:** you need to interpret an existing production model — these are new models trained from scratch at small scale.

**Open problem and bar.** The open question is whether weight sparsity yields circuits that are *both* small and faithful at useful scale. The evidence that would settle it: circuits that keep their size advantage over dense models while passing (1) a **nonsense-task control** (pruning cannot reach low loss on a task the model does not perform), (2) **out-of-distribution checks** where two candidate mechanisms disagree (e.g. same-gender IOI), and (3) a stronger faithfulness test than loss-versus-circuit-size, such as frozen-LayerNorm pruning or causal scrubbing — and doing so past the tens-of-millions-of-nonzero-parameters ceiling.

## SAEBench

Aliases: `sae-bench` on PyPI (v0.6.0), `adamkarvonen/SAEBench` on GitHub, "SAE Bench", "the SAE benchmark". Paper: arXiv **2503.09532**, "SAEBench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Sparse Autoencoders in Language Model Interpretability".

**What it is.** A suite of **eight evaluations** for SAEs, so you can compare architectures and sparsity levels on something other than the reconstruction/sparsity Pareto curve. The evals: **Feature Absorption**, **AutoInterp**, **L0 / Loss Recovered**, **RAVEL**, **Spurious Correlation Removal (SCR)**, **Targeted Probe Perturbation (TPP)**, **Sparse Probing** (plus an SAE-Probes variant), and **Unlearning**.

```bash
pip install sae-bench
```

Run a single eval over a family of SAEs (verbatim from the repo README):

```bash
python -m sae_bench.evals.sparse_probing.main \
    --sae_regex_pattern "sae_bench_pythia70m_sweep_standard_ctx128_0712" \
    --sae_block_pattern "blocks.4.hook_resid_post__trainer_10" \
    --model_name pythia-70m-deduped
```

Results are written under `eval_results/<eval_name>`.

**Reliability caveat: don't rank SAEs on TPP or SCR (as of 2026-10-14).** David Chanin, "Are Sparse Autoencoder Benchmarks Reliable?" (arXiv:2605.18229, 2026-05-18; code `decoderesearch/saebench-reliability-audit`, MIT) audits SAEBench three ways: reseed noise on a fixed canonical Gemma Scope SAE, correlation with ground truth on synthetic SAEs built on SynthSAEBench, and discriminability across training trajectories. Findings (abstract): "two of these metrics, Targeted Probe Perturbation (TPP) and Spurious Correlation Removal (SCR), fail multiple lenses at their canonical settings and should not be used to evaluate SAEs"; the other metrics "show higher reseed noise and lower discriminability than the field assumes"; and "the sae-probes variant of k-sparse probing is the most reliable metric we tested, but even sae-probes struggles to separate variants of the same SAE architecture". Practical reading: report the spread across several seeds for every SAEBench number, lean on the `sae-probes` k-sparse probing variant, and treat a TPP or SCR win (including ICA Lens's TPP result above) as weak evidence. Reseed run, verbatim from the audit README:

```bash
python -m saebench_audit.runners.reseed \
    --sae-release gemma-scope-2b-pt-res-canonical \
    --sae-id layer_12/width_65k/canonical \
    --seeds 42 123 456 789 2024 \
    --output-dir results/reseed
```

**Dominance-aware evaluation (DAME).** Zhang & Wang, "Rethinking SAE Evaluation: An Atomic Interpretable Unit Framework Reveals Hidden Polysemanticity and Redundancy" (arXiv:2610.30474, 2026-10-21; preprint): across "hundreds of SAEs" of several architectures, "the most dominant features, those contributing most to model computation, are systematically less interpretable and more redundant" — so averages over all features can look monosemantic while the features that matter are not. They propose DAME (Dominance-Aware Monosemanticity Evaluation) and recommend dominance-aware checks before using SAE features for attribution, circuit tracing or intervention. Practical reading: report interpretability for the top features by contribution, not only the average.

When not to over-read it: it is not a reason to drop SAEBench — the audit found one usable metric and judged the rest at canonical settings, and its own conclusion is that "the field needs better SAE benchmarks".

**When to use it:**

- You trained a new SAE variant and need to show it is better at *something a person cares about*, not just at reconstruction-per-L0. This is the standard the field now expects.
- You are choosing between pretrained SAEs for a downstream project and want a principled pick.
- You want a ready-made downstream task (unlearning, sparse probing) rather than inventing one. (SCR and TPP are also ready-made, but see the reliability caveat below before ranking SAEs on them.)

**When *not* to use it:**

- You just want L0 and loss-recovered — SAELens computes those directly, and the harness is overhead.
- Your SAE is for a model/hook not covered by the regex-selected families; you'll be writing config plumbing before you get a number.

**Pitfalls:**

- **Regex selection is silent when it matches nothing.** A typo in `--sae_regex_pattern` gives you an empty run rather than an error. Check how many SAEs were selected before interpreting an eval that finished suspiciously fast.
- **Feature absorption and SCR need probing data** and will download datasets on first run; a sandboxed/offline box fails here first.
- **Don't compare across sparsity levels without saying so.** Most of these metrics move with L0; a comparison table where SAEs sit at different L0 is not a comparison of architectures.
- **Unlearning eval ≠ unlearning research.** It measures whether SAE feature clamping suppresses a capability, which is one narrow probe — see [`unlearning.md`](../alignment-science/unlearning.md) for what the unlearning literature demands.

## SynthSAEBench (synthetic ground-truth features)

Aliases: "SynthSAEBench", "SynthSAEBench-16k", "synthetic SAE benchmark", `sae_lens.synthetic`, Chanin & Garriga-Alonso 2026. Paper: arXiv **2602.14687** (v2, 2026-07-13). Ships inside **SAELens** (docs page "SynthSAEBench" at `decoderesearch.github.io/SAELens`), not as a separate package.

**What it is.** A synthetic "model" whose activations are generated from 16,384 known ground-truth features in a 768-dimensional space, with realistic complications built in: Zipfian firing frequencies, a feature hierarchy (128 roots, branching factor 4, depth 3, mutually exclusive children), low-rank correlations and superposition. Because the true features and their firings are known, an SAE can be scored directly — **MCC** (mean correlation coefficient: Hungarian-matched mean |cosine| between decoder columns and true features), feature **uniqueness**, **precision/recall/F1** of latent firings, explained variance, L0, dead latents and shrinkage. The authors' motivation: "Current LLM-based SAE benchmarks are too noisy to differentiate architectural improvements". It reproduces known LLM SAE phenomena (reconstruction and latent quality disagree; poor SAE probing; a precision–recall trade-off set by L0) and identified a new failure mode: Matching Pursuit SAEs exploit superposition noise to improve reconstruction without learning the true features.

```python
# pip install sae-lens   (from the SAELens SynthSAEBench docs page)
from sae_lens.synthetic import SyntheticModel
model = SyntheticModel.from_pretrained("decoderesearch/synth-sae-bench-16k-v2", device="cuda")
```
Training goes through `SyntheticSAERunner(SyntheticSAERunnerConfig(...))`; the docs recommend SAE width 4,096, 200M samples, learning rate 3e-4, batch size 1,024, and report final `mcc`, `explained_variance`, `uniqueness` and `classification.f1_score`.

**Pitfall: `seed=None` was deterministic before SAELens 6.54.13.** `random_firing_probabilities` built a fresh `torch.Generator()` even without a seed, which starts from a fixed default seed, so it "returns the same probabilities on every call" and ignores `torch.manual_seed` (PR #776). `SyntheticModel` passes `cfg.seed` through, so unseeded synthetic models shared one firing-rate draw while their dictionaries and magnitudes varied; "Averaging SAE metrics over unseeded synthetic models averages over one firing-rate draw". Upgrade (fixed in v6.54.13) or set seeds explicitly. Seeded runs are bit-identical before and after.

**Pitfall: use `-v2`.** The docs say v1 of SynthSAEBench-16k was uploaded without `scale_children_by_parent=True`, so it does not match the paper's model; v2 fixes only that. Results on `decoderesearch/synth-sae-bench-16k` (v1) are not comparable with the paper.

**When to use it:** iterating on an SAE *architecture or training trick* — a run takes about 20 minutes on one GPU (per chanind, below), and a method that fails here, where the linear representation hypothesis holds by construction, has little hope on an LLM. **When *not* to use it:** as evidence that an SAE is good on a real model — it is the paper's "controlled lower-bound test"; pair it with SAEBench.

**Autonomous agent result on SynthSAEBench.** chanind, *Letting Claude do Autonomous Research to Improve SAEs* (LessWrong, 2026-03-10; MATS 7.1; code and the `TASK.md` prompt at `chanind/claude-auto-research-synthsaebench`, MIT). Claude Code (Claude Opus 4.6), run unattended overnight in a loop of "research sprints" (idea → implement → run → report) with light steering through `TASK.md`, raised F1 on SynthSAEBench-16k "from 0.88 to 0.95"; within another day, with occasional input, it matched the logistic-regression probe ceiling of 0.97, and MCC rose from 0.78 to 0.84. The resulting "LISTA-Matryoshka" SAE combines a one-iteration LISTA encoder (adapted by Claude from a 2010 sparse-coding paper), annealing K downward during training, detaching inner Matryoshka levels, a TERM loss, and sorting Matryoshka levels by firing frequency. Caveats the author states: the gains are **not yet verified on LLM SAEs** (LISTA with eta = 0.3 "seems to break on LLMs"), some gains may come from hill-climbing one synthetic model, and validating a single change on SAEBench needs multiple seeds and L0s and can cost $1,000+ in compute; Claude also once declared an idea failed when an implementation bug meant the sprint had tested nothing. **Regime:** a long, largely unattended single-agent loop with a fast, unambiguous metric; transfer to short interactive researcher+agent sessions, or to slow noisy metrics, is untested.

**Open problem and bar.** An SAE architecture counts as an improvement when it beats the strongest SynthSAEBench baselines (the Matryoshka SAE in the paper; LISTA-Matryoshka since chanind's post) on MCC and F1 at matched L0 **and** that gain carries over to SAEBench on a real LLM across several seeds and L0 values — the step that, as of the post, had not been shown for the LISTA changes.

## Common SAE pitfalls (cross-cutting)

- **Reconstruction loss is not the goal.** A trivially-perfect-reconstruction SAE can still be uninterpretable. Check L0, % alive features, and qualitative feature inspection.
- **Dead features.** A nontrivial fraction of features may never activate. SAELens and sparsify both have "ghost grad" / "auxiliary loss" mechanisms; use them or expect 30%+ dead features.
- **Width matters.** A 16k-feature SAE on Gemma-2-2B residual stream is "narrow" by 2026 standards; 65k–1M is now common. Width changes feature granularity.
- **"Feature splitting."** As you increase width, what was one feature splits into several finer-grained ones. Don't assume a feature with the same description in two SAE widths is "the same."
- **Don't compare absolute reconstruction loss across models / hook points.** It's only meaningful within a fixed setup.
- **Sandwich-norm hook trap (Gemma 2/3/4, OLMo 2/3).** "MLP output" is two tensors on these models; Gemma Scope MLP SAEs read the post-`post_feedforward_layernorm` residual contribution, and fed the raw MLP output they look dead (FVU 9.8, L0 8 vs FVU 0.26, L0 81 on gemma-2-2b layer 4). Check FVU and L0 on both candidates — see [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#the-sandwich-norm-hook-trap-gemma-234-olmo-23).
- **Silent loader and saver bugs exist; sanity-check explained variance and L0 after loading.** SAELens < 6.55.1 loaded Llama Scope SAEs with thresholds that ignored the decoder norm (L0 31.6 instead of ~48 at trained k = 50); SAELens < 6.55.0 spliced skip transcoders in without their skip connection; circuit-tracer ≤ 0.5.4 loads cached or local top-k transcoders with ReLU; SAELens < 6.54.2 transposed Gemma Scope 2 affine skip connections; SAELens ≤ 6.54.8 saved BatchTopK SAEs trained with activation-norm scaling with the threshold in the wrong units (mean L0 19.6 instead of k = 4 in the bug report); circuit-tracer ≤ 0.5.2 dropped CLT skip weights on save. Evaluation code has had silent bugs too: SAELens < 6.54.14 inflated CE-recovered / KL scores with special tokens excluded, and Delphi < 0.1.4 leaked explainer examples into its test set. A negative or near-zero explained variance, or an L0 far from the training target, on in-distribution text from a dictionary is a loading/saving problem until shown otherwise.
- **Single-seed benchmark numbers.** SAEBench metrics carry more reseed noise than assumed, and TPP/SCR fail an audit (see [SAEBench](#saebench)); report seed spread.
- **Reconstruction converges long before features are reproducible.** Ghosh, Mathai, Babu & Agarwal, "Diminishing Returns of Data in Sparse Autoencoders" (arXiv:2610.20680, 2026-10-14; four architectures, three models, 5M to 500M training tokens): "reconstruction often approaches the full-data reference at substantially smaller budgets, whereas feature reproducibility continues to improve with data. For Gemma-2-2B BatchTopK, the fraction of strictly matched features rises from 2% at 5M tokens to 29% at 50M." A cheap SAE can be fine for reconstruction-only uses; a claim about a *specific* feature needs a retrain (another seed or data sample) and a one-to-one match check.
- **A test that rejects nulls can still accept empty features.** Wu, "Null Controls Are Necessary but Not Sufficient" (arXiv:2610.22445, 2026-10-15; NeurIPS 2026 XAI4Science workshop): "In sparse autoencoders, enrichment rejects all 16 tested nulls, yet independent ablation supports 45% of evaluable enriched features, compared with 0.5% of matched controls." Pair a null-rejection test with an independent intervention (ablate or steer the feature and check the predicted consequence) before calling a feature validated.
- **Massive activations make "variance explained" and "top features by ablation damage" unreliable on anisotropic models (2026-11).** One direction (the first principal component) can hold up to 99.9% of residual-stream variance; SAE seeds rebuild it from different features, and those features top the importance ranking. Details and the fix in the next subsection.
- **"Fraction of features preserved" has no natural scale — calibrate it against a reseed.** On a GPT-2 activation cache, retraining a TopK SAE after rounding the cache to fp16 kept 60.6% of the original features, changing one value in a million by the smallest fp32 step kept 70.5%, and changing only the random seed kept 23.0% (Yeung, arXiv:2611.08551; details under "How many unique activations does SAE training need?" below). Any claim that a change (compression, a finetune, a new training recipe) "preserves the features", or that two SAEs "found the same feature", needs the seed-to-seed floor and the identical-input ceiling beside it.
- **A base-model SAE fine-tuned onto a shifted model can reconstruct well and still lose feature-level meaning.** Gupte, Cai, Zhang & Khalili, "Representational Change Under Self-Consuming Training: A Sparse Autoencoder Analysis" (arXiv:2611.21348, 2026-11-16; Pythia-160M and Gemma-2-2B over five generations of training on the lineage's own synthetic text, each compared with a matched real-data control): zero-shot SAE reconstruction and sparsity degrade progressively, and "Fine-tuning largely restores reconstruction, but not feature-level usefulness: task-relevant information remains accessible in the full latent space while becoming less concentrated in individual features." The clean effect is on Pythia-160M; on Gemma-2-2B the feature-level effects "vary across layers and metrics". Practical reading for anyone reusing a base-model SAE on a finetune (see [`model-diffing.md`](model-diffing.md)): after adapting the SAE, check with sparse probing (how few latents carry a known concept), not only with reconstruction.

### Why are my most important SAE features different on every seed? (massive activations)

Aliases: massive activations, outlier dimensions, attention-sink activations, PC1 dominance, first principal component, anisotropy, "PC1 tiling", content variance, "variance explained is 99.9% but features are garbage", `nathandelisle/massive-saes`.

**The result.** Delisle & Tan (University of Chicago), "Massive Activations Confound Sparse Autoencoders" (arXiv:2611.13548, 2026-11-10; the PDF carries a NeurIPS 2026 footer; code `nathandelisle/massive-saes`, MIT; SAEs at `huggingface.co/nathandelisle/massive-saes`). Massive activations — outlier dimensions written by early feed-forward layers that implement attention sinks — "cause up to 99.9% of activation variance to lie along the first principal component (PC1)" of the residual stream (70–99.9% in middle layers, per the introduction). Two consequences for the two numbers people report first about a sparse autoencoder (SAE):

- **Ablation-importance rankings are dominated by seed artifacts.** "Because the PC1 component has no unique sparse decomposition, each SAE run rebuilds it from a different set of features. These features dominate importance rankings: an average of 47 of the 50 most important features are unmatched in a separate run, compared with about 27 of a random 50." In five highly anisotropic models the 50 most important features had mean pairwise sharing rates of only 5–11% across seeds, against 47–63% for the whole dictionary — the most important features were the *least* reproducible.
- **Variance explained hides content error.** Reconstruction error is variance-weighted, so the score mostly measures how well PC1 is reconstructed: "A dictionary can also report 99.96% of variance explained while leaving 33% of the variance outside PC1 unexplained" (the paper's Qwen-2.5-7B SAEs). The error on the variance outside PC1 is understated by roughly 1 / (1 − PC1's share of variance).

**Which SAEs.** In the paper's experiments TopK, BatchTopK and JumpReLU with scheduled thresholds show the inversion between importance and reproducibility, "whereas ReLU+ℓ1 does not"; adding a magnitude penalty to a TopK SAE (GPT-2, block 6, five seeds) raised top-50 sharing from 6.4% to 94.9% with under 1% change in sparsity or reconstruction error. SAEs were trained on GPT-2, Pythia-70M/160M/1.4B/6.9B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B/70B and Gemma-2-2B/9B; the severity differs by model: the top 50 are shared less than the whole dictionary in every model where PC1 holds at least 93% of the variance, the five models where 43.5–48 of the top 50 fail to recur are GPT-2, Qwen-2.5-7B and Pythia-160M/1.4B/6.9B, and "Neither Gemma model shows the inversion under any objective". Released dictionaries are affected too: FVU outside PC1 is "approximately 2.1× total FVU in Gemma Scope 2", though the paper says normalization substantially reduces the effect in other released dictionaries.

**The fix they propose.** Train the SAE on activations with the PC1 projection removed and add the projection back to the SAE's output at inference. "In affected models, the top-50 feature sharing rate rises from 4-39.5% to 73-99.5%. At the same TopK sparsity, the resulting SAE degrades the model up to 44% less and reduces content reconstruction error by up to 33.9%."

**What to do (tips).** Before trusting an SAE on a new model and layer: compute PC1's share of activation variance at that site; report FVU (fraction of variance unexplained) both in total and with PC1 projected out; when ranking features by ablation damage, check each top feature's decoder alignment with PC1 and whether it recurs in a second seed; for an existing SAE, use the paper's post-hoc variant — exclude features with squared PC1 alignment ≥ 0.01 from the importance ranking (0.72–3.92% of alive features in their runs), after which the top 50 remaining features were shared at 77.5–96% across seeds, without retraining. Dropping the first token position is not a general fix: it "removes PC1 dominance where massive activations are mainly present there, as in Llama-3.1-8B (97% to 3%), while Qwen-2.5-7B remains at 99.7%". **When this does not apply:** models and sites where PC1 holds a small share of variance (in Pythia-1.4B the inversion disappears at the final layer); vanilla ReLU + L1 SAEs (not affected in the paper's tests). Even with PC1 separated, the dictionary-wide cross-seed sharing rate stays between 44% and 68%, so this fixes the ranking, not SAE seed instability in general. The same anisotropy shows up in lens work on Gemma-4-31B (see [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#pitfalls-with-searchable-symptoms)).

### How many unique activations does SAE training need? (replay buffers, compressed caches)

Aliases: activation buffer, replay buffer, activation cache, cached activations, activation storage, "disk for SAE activations", fp16 / bf16 / int4 / int8 activation cache, quantized activations, activation-cache compression.

**Replaying a small buffer is nearly as good as fresh activations, on reconstruction-type metrics.** Koriagin, Balagansky & Gavrilov, "Scaling Laws for SAE Training Data" (arXiv:2611.07756, 2026-11-05; preprint). With the total number of activations shown to the optimizer held fixed, quality "improves rapidly as the buffer grows, then reaches a plateau where replaying the buffer is nearly as effective as using fresh activations throughout training". Their rule: "choose the smallest buffer that reaches the quality plateau and replay it for the remaining budget. In our experiments, this reduces activation storage by 8–64× with negligible quality loss." Tested on Llama-3.1-8B residual activations from FineWeb (layers 5, 15 and 25), Qwen3-1.7B / 4B / 8B, and BatchTopK, TopK and JumpReLU SAEs; metrics were explained variance, reconstruction MSE, feature absorption, automated interpretability and sparse probing. In one comparison (32k dictionary, 640M total activations, Llama-3.1-8B layer 15) the three architectures saturated at 40M unique activations. Limits the authors state: the full grid is one dataset and one model at fixed sparsity, "Extrapolation is less accurate for undertrained wider dictionaries, and stricter EV tolerances can require larger buffers", and SAEBench covers only the BatchTopK sweep at layer 15. **What it does not test:** whether *the same features* come out. A separate study on this page found that reconstruction converges long before cross-seed feature reproducibility does (arXiv:2610.20680, in the pitfalls above), so a replay buffer sized on the explained-variance plateau is a storage saving for reconstruction-quality goals, not evidence that feature identity is unaffected. No code link was found in the PDF.

**Compressing the cache changes which features you get — measure against a reseed.** Abraham Yeung, "How Small Can We Go? Calibrating Activation-Cache Compression for SAE Training" (arXiv:2611.08551, 2026-11-05; single author). Two tests: pass the compressed cache through the original fp32-trained SAE (frozen-SAE test), and retrain an SAE on the compressed cache and count how many original features still exist (retraining test). In the retraining test "there is no clear scale for retained features. On a GPT-2 cache, rounding everything to fp16 keeps 60.6% of features, but changing one value in a million by the smallest step fp32 allows (a change eight orders of magnitude smaller) keeps only 70.5%. Meanwhile, changing the random seed keeps 23.0%." So the paper calibrates per site: "retraining on identical bytes sets the ceiling, and a reseed sets the floor." Findings with TopK SAEs on GPT-2 small (layer 6; k = 32 of 12,288 latents; 20M OpenWebText tokens) and Pythia-1.4B (layers 8 and 16): "naïve per-token int4 loses more features than a reseed everywhere we tested, as do PCA and random projection on GPT-2. Standardizing each channel before quantizing brings int4 back above the floor in terms of retained features, at the same raw file size. At our smallest budget, a bf16 forward pass, which some pipelines use to save memory, loses slightly more features than rotated 8-bit storage. Calibrating a site costs four training runs and two evaluation passes." Scope: small models and small dictionaries at "research-scale budgets"; whether the ordering of codecs holds for large SAEs on 8B+ models is untested.

**Practical reading.** For reconstruction-quality goals on a storage-limited cluster, a replayed buffer and a standardized-then-quantized cache are both reasonable — record the buffer size, dtype and codec with the SAE. For any claim about *specific* features, train a second seed and report the reseed floor next to whatever you changed. In SAELens the buffer size is `n_sequences_in_buffer` (called `n_batches_in_buffer` before v6.56.0), counted in sequences. Extraction-side dtype and storage choices are in [`serving-and-activations.md`](serving-and-activations.md).

## Common questions

### How do I load a Gemma Scope SAE?

```python
from sae_lens import SAE
sae, cfg, sparsity = SAE.from_pretrained(
    release="gemma-scope-2b-pt-res",
    sae_id="layer_20/width_16k/average_l0_71",
    device="cuda",
)
```
Gemma Scope is the open Gemma-2 SAE release; `release` and `sae_id` come from the SAELens registry. List available SAEs via `sae_lens.loading.pretrained_saes_directory.get_pretrained_saes_directory()`.

### What's the difference between TopK and JumpReLU SAEs?

**TopK SAE**: enforces sparsity directly — only the top K activations per token are kept, the rest are zeroed. No L1 penalty needed; L0 = K by construction. **JumpReLU SAE**: uses a learned threshold per feature; activations below threshold go to zero. Both are 2024-era replacements for vanilla L1-penalty SAEs and produce cleaner features for the same sparsity level.

### My SAE has too many dead features — what do I do?

Dead features (never activate on any input) are common, often 30%+ in vanilla SAEs. Mitigations: use **TopK** or **JumpReLU** SAEs (much fewer dead features by construction); enable **ghost-grad / auxiliary loss** in SAELens or sparsify (resurrects dying features mid-training); use higher learning rate early in training.

### Should I use SAELens or EleutherAI sparsify for training?

**SAELens** if you want plug-and-play with the TransformerLens / HuggingFace ecosystem and many architectures (GatedSAE, JumpReLU, TopK). **EleutherAI sparsify** for fastest TopK SAE training, multi-GPU via DDP, and transcoder support (`--transcode` flag). Install with `pip install eai-sparsify` (the unprefixed `sparsify` on PyPI belongs to Neural Magic's unrelated tool). For Matryoshka BatchTopK or feature-circuits-style research, **dictionary_learning** (Sam Marks).

### Can I steer using SAE features?

Yes. Pattern: encode the residual stream into SAE features, modify the feature you want (clamp, scale, ablate), decode back, continue forward pass. SAELens has hooks for this. See [`steering.md`](steering.md) "SAE feature steering." Pitfalls: reconstruction error compounds; feature splitting at higher SAE widths may mean your "deception feature" is now five features. **Pick the feature by its intervention effect, not by correlation:** Guo, Li & Chen (arXiv:2610.15850, 2026-10-11; four model–SAE pairs) report that for single-latent ablations with nonzero effects, a first-order gradient estimate ("intervention attribution") predicted the sign of the effect in 91–97% of cases versus 17–35% for probe-implied changes, and attribution-selected sets gave larger held-out ablation effects than sets chosen by correlation, rank tests or probe coefficients.

### Are my Delphi detection / fuzzing scores inflated?

If they came from `eai-delphi` before 0.1.4 with the default sampler config, probably: 10–20 of the 50 default test examples had also been shown to the explainer (v0.1.4 release notes, 2026-11-02), which inflates detection and fuzzing scores, and simulator correlations could be NaN on transformers ≥5. Upgrade to `eai-delphi>=0.1.4` (0.2.0 is current) and re-score before comparing. See [Delphi](#delphi-eleutherai).

### Is my SAE's "CE loss recovered" too high?

If you computed it with SAELens `run_evals` before 6.54.14 with special tokens excluded (the default), yes: the zero-ablation reference also zeroed BOS, inflating the ablation loss and both `ce_loss_score` and `kl_div_score` (in one GPT-2 example the unexplained CE share read 1.4% instead of 2.4%). Upgrade to `sae-lens>=6.54.14` and recompute. See [SAELens](#saelens).

### My saved BatchTopK SAE fires far more features than k — why?

If you trained it in SAELens with `normalize_activations="expected_average_only_in"` and saved it with SAELens ≤ 6.54.8, the JumpReLU `threshold` was saved in the wrong units (issue #763; fixed in v6.54.9, 2026-10-20). With an activation scaling factor below 1 too many features fire, above 1 too few. Upgrade to `sae-lens>=6.54.10`, re-save from a training checkpoint or retrain, and check that the mean L0 on raw activations equals the training *k*. See [SAELens](#saelens).

### What is feature splitting?

When you train a wider SAE on the same model, what was one feature in a narrower SAE often becomes 2–10 finer-grained features. So "feature 3217 in the 16k SAE" is *not* the same thing as "feature 3217 in the 65k SAE." Don't compare feature IDs across widths; compare via cosine similarity or activation pattern.

### My SAE features look uninterpretable — what's wrong?

Most common causes: (1) **hook point mismatch** — different SAEs are trained at `hook_resid_pre` vs `hook_resid_post` vs `hook_mlp_out`; loading at the wrong hook gives garbage. On Gemma 2/3/4 and OLMo 2/3 there is a second trap: the raw MLP output (`mlp.hook_out`, HF `model.model.layers[i].mlp`) is not the post-norm contribution (`hook_mlp_out`) that Gemma Scope MLP SAEs read ([details](mech-interp.md#the-sandwich-norm-hook-trap-gemma-234-olmo-23)). (2) **Tokenizer / chat-template mismatch** — pretrained SAEs were trained on raw text or specific chat format; applying to other formats degrades reconstruction. (3) **Width too narrow** for the model — 16k features on a 2B model is small by 2026 standards. (4) **A loader or splicing bug** — for Gemma Scope 2 affine transcoders upgrade to `sae-lens>=6.55.0` (transposed skip before 6.54.2; skip connection dropped when spliced before 6.55.0); for Llama Scope SAEs upgrade to `sae-lens>=6.55.1` (thresholds ignored the decoder norm). (5) **You are looking at the "most important" features on an anisotropic model** — features ranked top by ablation damage can be seed-specific pieces of the first principal component ([massive activations](#why-are-my-most-important-sae-features-different-on-every-seed-massive-activations)).

### My Gemma Scope 2 transcoder has negative explained variance — why?

If its ID ends in `_affine` and you loaded it with SAELens before 6.54.2, the skip connection was applied transposed (SAELens PR #761: −16.4 instead of 0.807 on 270m-pt layer 15). `pip install -U "sae-lens>=6.55.0"` (6.54.2 fixes the transpose; 6.55.0 also fixes spliced runs dropping the skip connection) and reload from the original `google/gemma-scope-2-*` repo — a copy re-saved in SAELens format keeps the transposed matrix.

### Can I trust SAEBench's TPP and SCR scores?

Not for ranking SAEs, per Chanin's audit (arXiv:2605.18229): Targeted Probe Perturbation and Spurious Correlation Removal fail reseed, synthetic-ground-truth and training-trajectory checks at their canonical settings. The `sae-probes` k-sparse probing variant was the most reliable metric tested. Report several seeds for every SAEBench number.

### Is there something cheaper than training an SAE for a first look?

**ICA Lens** (`pip install icalens`; arXiv:2606.11722) fits independent component analysis to a layer's activations — "substantially more compute-efficient to fit than an SAE dictionary" (README) — and ships an interactive token-level explorer and published lenses for GPT-2 small and Qwen3.5-2B. Standard ICA yields no more components than the activation dimension (whether `icalens` relaxes this was not checked), so treat it as a first lens and a baseline, not a replacement for an overcomplete dictionary. Neuronpedia publishes pre-fitted ICA lenses in SAELens format for six models including Qwen3.6-27B (`neuronpedia/icalens-*`, 2026-11-07). A second no-training option on models with a released J-lens: read raw MLP neurons' output vectors through the lens, or browse a public neuron catalog (see [`mech-interp.md`](mech-interp.md#how-do-i-read-an-mlp-neuron-through-a-lens-neuron-output-vectors-j-share)).

### How much disk do I need for SAE training activations?

Possibly 8–64× less than a fresh-activations run: on Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen3 models, replaying a buffer sized at the explained-variance plateau gave "negligible quality loss" on reconstruction, absorption, auto-interp and sparse-probing metrics (arXiv:2611.07756). Cross-seed feature identity was not tested there, and compressing the cache (fp16, int4, PCA) changes which features a retrained SAE finds by amounts that only mean something next to a reseed baseline (arXiv:2611.08551). See [How many unique activations does SAE training need?](#how-many-unique-activations-does-sae-training-need-replay-buffers-compressed-caches).

### Are there pretrained SAEs for Gemma 3?

Yes — **Gemma Scope 2** (`google/gemma-scope-2-{270m,1b,4b,12b,27b}-{pt,it}`, December 2025): SAEs, transcoders, crosscoders and cross-layer transcoders, loadable through SAELens (e.g. `release="gemma-scope-2-4b-it-resid_post"`). For Gemma 2 use the original Gemma Scope (`gemma-scope-2b-pt-res` etc.). Neuronpedia hosts a browser at `neuronpedia.org/gemma-scope-2`.

### `import sae_lens` fails with `cannot import name 'HookedTransformer' from 'transformer_lens'` — why?

TransformerLens 4.0.0 (2026-09-21) removed `HookedTransformer`, and SAELens before 6.51.2 had no upper bound on TransformerLens. Upgrade to `sae-lens>=6.55.0` (2026-11-05), which supports TransformerLens 4.x (`transformer-lens>=3.0.0,<5.0.0`; latest 6.56.1): on TL 4 use `SAETransformerBridge` or `model_class_name="TransformerBridge"`, because `HookedSAETransformer` exists only with TransformerLens 3.x installed. To keep old `HookedSAETransformer` code unchanged, `pip install "transformer-lens<4"`.

### My Llama Scope SAE has a much lower L0 than its trained k — why?

If you loaded it through SAELens before 6.55.1, the JumpReLU threshold the loader built ignored the decoder norm, so "loaded SAEs fire about 35 % fewer latents than in training" (PR #782: on `l15r_8x`, L0 31.55 instead of 48.36 at trained k = 50; fixed in v6.55.1, 2026-11-10). `pip install -U "sae-lens>=6.55.1"`, reload, and re-run any feature-level analysis made on the old active sets. See [SAELens](#saelens).

### How is parameter decomposition different from an SAE or transcoder?

SAEs and transcoders learn a dictionary over *activations*; parameter decomposition (SPD, VPD; `goodfire-ai/param-decomp`) splits the *weight matrices* into sparse rank-one subcomponents that sum to the original parameters. It has so far been demonstrated on a 4-layer, ~67M-parameter language model (paper now arXiv:2610.20748), and two independent audits of the released decomposition found aggregated explanations drift from the original model's outputs and that the evaluation adversary's damaging masks transfer to unseen text — treat it as a research direction, not a drop-in replacement for SAELens.

### Are raw MLP neurons interpretable after all? Do I still need an SAE or transcoder?

Still contested as of 2026-11-23, but with more evidence on the neuron side. New in 2026-11: Goodfire's technical report "Neurons as an Interpretable Basis" (2026-11-10; Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) reports that keeping only the top 320 of 4,096 routed neurons per token per layer costs "1.2-2.2 points" on log-likelihood benchmarks without generated reasoning (reasoning benchmarks need a light fine-tune to recover), and that agent-written neuron descriptions support detection and simulation; Anthropic Fellows' "Returning to the Neuron Basis" (2026-11-23) reads neuron output vectors through a J-lens on four open models and finds neurons adding two unrelated concepts to be about 1% or fewer of sampled middle-layer signed directions, with neurons chosen from their descriptions and readouts beating SAE latents chosen from their descriptions at concept detection — while stating that lens readability is only a proxy and that readouts matched top-activating text in 31% of ratings for the majority of neurons. Neither is independently replicated. Earlier evidence for neurons: many transcoder features match single MLP neurons, and those features have a stable correspondence with the underlying MLP that the rest lack; sign-split TopK neurons with a trained linear output map beat transcoders on downstream KL while losing on reconstruction (Shabalin & Piotrowski, arXiv:2610.24521); neuron-basis circuits of ~100 neurons control subject-verb agreement (Arora et al., arXiv:2601.22594, `TransluceAI/circuits`); Cammarata (Goodfire, 2026-10-15) estimates ~90% of Qwen3-32B neuron poles have one main job, a preliminary, threshold-dependent figure from the essay (the 2026-11 report above studies a different model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B). Evidence against over-reading it: Leo Gao (LessWrong, 2026-10-17) argues top-activating examples cannot establish an explanation and asks for simulation scored by downstream KL on a large dataset. Practical answer: run a neuron baseline (both poles, split by sign regime) next to any dictionary; transcoders still win on fine-grained features such as bigrams. Details in "Should I use SAEs at all?" above.
